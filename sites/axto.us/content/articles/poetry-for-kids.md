Poetry is often the first literature children meet. Long before they can read, they bounce to nursery rhymes, clap along to playground chants and giggle at silly verses. Yet as children get older, poems often slip out of their reading lives, squeezed out by chapter books, homework and the sense that poetry is difficult or only for school.

That is a loss, because poems are one of the most efficient and enjoyable ways to build reading skills. A good poem is short, musical, packed with interesting words and meant to be read again and again. This guide explains what poetry does for young readers, which kinds of poems suit each age, and simple ways to make poems part of family life.

## What poetry does for young readers

### It tunes children's ears to the sounds of language

Rhyme, rhythm, alliteration and repetition draw attention to the sounds inside words. Hearing that *cat* rhymes with *hat*, or that *slippery snakes slide* all start with the same sound, builds phonological awareness, one of the foundations of learning to read. (See [why nursery rhymes matter](/guides/nursery-rhymes-phonological-awareness/).)

### It builds fluency

Fluent reading is accurate, smooth and expressive. Poems are ideal for practicing it because they are short enough to reread several times and are meant to be read aloud with feeling. Rereading a favorite poem until it sounds just right is a natural form of the repeated reading that teachers use to build fluency. (See [how to build reading fluency at home](/guides/reading-fluency-at-home/).)

### It grows vocabulary and imagination

Poets choose words carefully. A single poem can introduce precise verbs, vivid adjectives and unusual comparisons, all in a few lines. Figurative language, such as metaphor and personification, helps children see ordinary things in new ways and prepares them for the richer language of older books.

### It offers a complete experience in a few lines

For a child who finds long texts overwhelming, a poem is a complete piece of literature that can be read in a minute. That makes poetry especially valuable for [reluctant readers](/guides/reluctant-readers/) and for children who are still building reading stamina.

### It gives words to feelings

Many poems explore emotions such as worry, joy, anger, loneliness and wonder. Reading them can help children recognize and name their own feelings, and talk about experiences that are hard to put into words.

## Poems for every age

### Babies and toddlers

The best poetry for the youngest children is the kind you say, sing and do together: nursery rhymes, lullabies, finger plays and bouncing rhymes such as "Pat-a-Cake," "This Little Piggy" and "The Itsy Bitsy Spider." Babies love the rhythm and the closeness; toddlers love joining in with actions and the last word of each line. Board books of nursery rhymes are a good start, but you do not need a book at all.

### Preschool and kindergarten

Rhyming picture books are a natural bridge from nursery rhymes to written poetry. Books such as *Chicka Chicka Boom Boom* by Bill Martin Jr. and John Archambault, and the many rhyming books by Dr. Seuss, invite children to predict the next rhyme and to join in. Short, funny poems about animals, food and everyday life also work well, as do action rhymes that get children moving.

### Early elementary

This is the golden age for funny poems. Collections of light verse by poets such as Shel Silverstein (*Where the Sidewalk Ends*, *A Light in the Attic*) and Jack Prelutsky (*The New Kid on the Block*) have delighted generations of children with their wordplay and absurd situations. Nonsense poems by Edward Lear and Lewis Carroll's "Jabberwocky" show how much fun invented words can be. Classic collections such as Robert Louis Stevenson's *A Child's Garden of Verses* and A. A. Milne's *When We Were Very Young* offer gentler pleasures.

Children at this age also enjoy concrete poems, which are shaped like their subject, and riddle poems that ask the reader to guess what is being described.

### Older children

As children mature, they are ready for poems with more depth: free verse, poems about history and identity, and poems that leave room for interpretation. Langston Hughes's *The Dream Keeper and Other Poems* and Gwendolyn Brooks's *Bronzeville Boys and Girls* are classics written with young readers in mind.

Novels in verse, which tell a whole story through a sequence of poems, are especially popular with this age group. Kwame Alexander's *The Crossover*, a story about twin brothers and basketball, won the Newbery Medal. Jacqueline Woodson's memoir in verse, *Brown Girl Dreaming*, won the National Book Award. Sharon Creech's *Love That Dog* follows a boy who insists he cannot write poetry until he discovers that he can. These books often appeal to readers who think they do not like poetry, because they read like gripping stories with plenty of white space on the page.

## Funny poems are real poems

Some adults worry that silly poems are not "real" poetry. They are. Light verse depends on rhythm, rhyme, timing and clever word choice, the same skills used in serious poetry, and it teaches children how language can surprise and delight. A child who loves a ridiculous poem about a hungry monster is learning to pay attention to the sound and shape of words.

Funny poems are also a doorway. Children who first enjoy poetry for its humor are often willing to try quieter, more thoughtful poems later, especially if an adult they trust shares them with enthusiasm.

## How to read a poem aloud with your child

- **Read it twice.** The first time, just enjoy it. The second time, slow down and listen to the sounds and the rhythm.
- **Bring out the beat.** Exaggerate the rhythm, clap or tap along and let your voice rise and fall with the lines.
- **Leave gaps.** With a rhyming poem your child knows, pause before the rhyming word and let them fill it in.
- **Ask open questions.** Instead of quizzing, ask "What did you notice?", "Which word did you like best?" or "What picture did you see in your head?"
- **Don't over-explain.** Poems can be enjoyed without being fully understood. It is fine to say, "I'm not sure what that line means either. What do you think?"
- **Try different voices.** Read a poem in a whisper, in a booming voice or in a character's voice. Take turns reading lines, or read together in unison.

## Bringing poetry into everyday life

Poetry works best in small, frequent doses. A few ideas:

- **A poem a day or a week.** Read one poem at breakfast, at bedtime or in the car. Keep a poetry book where you will see it.
- **Fridge poems.** Copy a short poem onto a card and stick it on the fridge. Change it every week.
- **Lunch-box poems.** Tuck a short poem or a funny verse into your child's lunch box.
- **Poetry teatime.** Some families hold a weekly "poetry teatime" with a pot of tea or cocoa, a snack and a stack of poetry books, and everyone takes turns choosing and reading poems.
- **Poetry walks.** Read a poem about the weather, trees or the sea while you are outside experiencing them.
- **Learning poems by heart.** Choose a short poem and learn it together over a week, then perform it for the family or record a video for a grandparent.

In the United States, April is National Poetry Month, and many libraries and schools hold special events, including a day when people carry a favorite poem in their pocket to share. It is a good moment to borrow a stack of poetry books from your [public library](/guides/using-the-public-library/).

## Writing poems together

Writing poems lets children play with words without the pressure of a long piece of writing. Try these simple forms:

- **Acrostic poems:** write a name or word down the side of the page and start each line with one of its letters.
- **List poems:** a list of things on a theme, such as "Things that are blue" or "Sounds I hear at night."
- **"I am" poems:** each line begins with "I am," "I like," "I wonder" or "I wish."
- **Shape poems:** write the words in the shape of the subject, such as a poem about rain falling down the page.
- **Haiku:** short three-line poems about nature. In English, children are often taught a five-seven-five syllable pattern; traditional Japanese haiku count sound units rather than English syllables, so the pattern is a helpful guide rather than a strict rule.
- **Rhyming couplets:** two lines that rhyme, which can grow into a longer silly poem.

Accept invented spelling in young children's poems; the goal is playing with words and ideas. (See [early writing and invented spelling](/guides/early-writing-invented-spelling/).) Older children can revise and polish a favorite poem to share.

## Building a home poetry shelf

A small poetry collection goes a long way. Aim for a mix:

- a book of nursery rhymes and songs for the youngest children;
- a large anthology with many poets, which lets children sample different styles;
- a collection by one funny poet your child loves;
- a book of poems on a favorite subject, such as animals, sports or space;
- for older children, a novel in verse or a collection by a poet whose work reflects their interests or background.

Poetry books are easy to find second-hand and at library sales, which makes them a good fit for [building a home library on a budget](/guides/home-library-on-a-budget/).

## Frequently asked questions

### My child doesn't "get" poetry. Should I keep trying?

Yes, but change the type of poem rather than pushing harder. Many children who dislike poetry have mainly met poems that felt like puzzles to solve. Start with funny poems, sports poems or a novel in verse, read them aloud with enthusiasm, and do not quiz your child afterward. Enjoyment comes first.

### Do poems have to rhyme?

No. Rhyme is one tool poets use, but many poems use rhythm, repetition, imagery or the layout of words on the page instead. Young children often prefer rhyming poems, which is fine, but it is worth showing them that poems can work in many different ways.

### Is memorizing poems old-fashioned?

Memorizing a short poem is a valuable exercise. It builds memory, vocabulary and confidence, and it gives children a poem they can carry with them for life. Keep it playful and choose poems your child likes, rather than making it a chore.

### Are song lyrics poetry?

Song lyrics and poems are close relatives; both use rhythm, rhyme and imagery, and many songs began as poems. Talking about the words in your child's favorite songs can be a great way into poetry, especially for older children who think poems are not for them.

## In summary

Poetry builds the skills that strong readers need, including sound awareness, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension, in short, enjoyable pieces that children want to reread. Start with rhymes and songs, move on to funny poems and picture-book verse, and introduce richer poems and novels in verse as children grow. Read poems aloud, keep them part of everyday life, write them together and let enjoyment lead the way. A child who loves poems has a lifelong source of pleasure, comfort and language.
