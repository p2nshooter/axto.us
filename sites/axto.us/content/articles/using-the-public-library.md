The public library is the most powerful free resource a family can have for raising readers. It offers far more books than any family could buy, expert librarians who know children's books inside out, programs from baby story time to teen book clubs, digital collections available at home and a welcoming community space. Yet many families use only a fraction of what their library offers, or don't visit at all.

This guide describes what modern public libraries offer children and families, how to get started and how to make library visits a habit your children look forward to.

## What public libraries offer families

### Books, books and more books

Board books, picture books, early readers, decodable books, chapter books, graphic novels, nonfiction, poetry, books in many languages, audiobooks and magazines. Borrowing lets children explore freely without the cost of buying.

### Digital collections

Most libraries lend e-books and audiobooks through free apps you can use on phones, tablets and e-readers. Many also offer streaming video, online learning resources, language-learning programs and databases for homework research.

### Story times and programs

- **Baby and toddler story times** with songs, rhymes and books, which model reading for parents and build early literacy.
- **Preschool story times** with stories, crafts and activities.
- **Summer reading programs** with challenges, events and small prizes.
- **Book clubs, author visits, maker spaces, coding clubs and craft sessions** for older children.
- **Programs in multiple languages** in many communities.

### Librarian expertise

Children's librarians are trained to match children with books. They can suggest books for a child who loves trains, find decodable books for a beginning reader, recommend a series to hook a reluctant reader or help with a school project.

### Homework help

Many libraries offer homework help, tutoring programs, quiet study spaces, computers, printing and access to research databases.

### Accessibility resources

Large-print books, audiobooks, books in braille through national programs, and resources for children with reading difficulties, including high-interest, lower-level books.

### A community space

Libraries are free, safe, welcoming spaces where families can spend time together, meet other families and feel part of a reading community.

## Getting started

### Get library cards for everyone

Children can usually get their own library card with a parent's permission, often from birth. Having their own card gives children a sense of ownership and pride. Ask about policies: many libraries have eliminated late fines for children's materials, removing a major barrier.

### Take a tour

Ask a librarian to show you around the children's section: where picture books, early readers, chapter books, graphic novels and nonfiction are shelved, and how to use the catalog.

### Download the library app

Learn how to search the catalog, place holds, renew books and borrow e-books and audiobooks from home.

## Making library visits a habit

### Go regularly

A weekly or biweekly visit, same day and time, makes the library part of family routine. Children look forward to choosing new books.

### Let children choose freely

Give children time to browse and choose books they want, even if they seem too easy, too hard or unusual. Choice builds motivation. You can add a few books of your own choosing too.

### Use a library bag and a home basket

A dedicated bag for library books and a basket at home where they live between visits prevents lost books.

### Attend programs

Story times and events give children positive associations with the library and introduce books and activities they might not discover otherwise.

### Ask librarians for help

Don't hesitate to ask: "My son loves dinosaurs and is just starting to read. What do you recommend?" Librarians love these questions.

### Place holds for popular books

If your child wants the next book in a popular series, place a hold so it's waiting for you.

## Library etiquette for children

Teach children to:

- Handle books gently, turning pages carefully.
- Keep library books away from food, drinks and pets.
- Return books on time or renew them.
- Use quiet voices in quiet areas (children's areas are usually more relaxed).
- Put books back where they found them or in the return cart.

## When a library book is damaged or lost

Accidents happen. Tell the librarian; many libraries have reasonable policies for damaged or lost books, and some offer fine forgiveness programs. Don't let worry about damage keep you away.

## Libraries and reading struggles

For children who find reading hard, the library offers high-interest, lower-level books, audiobooks, graphic novels and a pressure-free environment. Librarians can help find books that match a child's interests and reading level. See [how to motivate a reluctant reader](/guides/reluctant-readers/).

## A first library visit plan

1. **Before you go:** look at the library's website together and pick one program to try.
2. **At the desk:** sign up for cards; let your child sign theirs if they can write their name.
3. **In the children's area:** give each child a target, such as "choose two books you'd like and one you've never tried."
4. **Ask the librarian** for one recommendation based on your child's interests.
5. **At home:** read one of the new books together that evening and put the rest in the library basket.
6. **Set the next date:** put the next visit on the family calendar.

## Myths about libraries

**"Libraries are outdated now that everything is online."** Libraries offer digital collections, technology and programs, and they remain the best way to get free access to physical books.

**"Library fines make it too expensive."** Many libraries have eliminated fines for children's books, and most offer forgiveness for families who need it.

**"The library is too quiet for kids."** Children's areas are designed for families, and story times can be lively and noisy.

## Digital library services

Most public libraries now offer far more than physical books. With a library card, families can often access:

- **Ebooks and audiobooks** through library apps, borrowed and returned automatically
- **Digital magazines and comics**, including popular children's titles
- **Streaming films and documentaries**, often educational
- **Online learning platforms** for language learning, homework help and test preparation
- **Research databases** with encyclopedias and articles written for students

Digital collections are especially helpful for families who live far from a branch, have limited time to visit or want audiobooks for long car journeys. Waitlists can be long for popular titles, so place holds early and explore less-requested gems while you wait. See [audiobooks for kids](/guides/audiobooks-for-kids/).

## Getting the most from librarians

Children's librarians are trained to match young readers with books. Instead of searching alone, try asking:

- "My daughter loved this book. What else might she like?"
- "Do you have funny books for a reluctant eight-year-old reader?"
- "What nonfiction do you have about volcanoes for a six-year-old?"
- "Can you recommend decodable books for a beginning reader?"

Librarians can also suggest books for specific situations, such as a new sibling, moving house, starting school or grief, and many keep lists of award winners and seasonal favorites.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can we borrow e-books for free?

Yes, most public libraries lend e-books and audiobooks free through apps.

### Is there a limit to how many books we can borrow?

Limits vary but are often generous. Ask your library.

### What if our library doesn't have a book we want?

Ask about requesting a purchase or borrowing it through interlibrary loan.

## In summary

The public library offers books, e-books, audiobooks, story times, summer reading programs, homework help and expert librarians, all free. Get library cards for everyone, visit regularly, let children choose freely, attend programs, ask librarians for help and use digital collections at home. A library habit is one of the best gifts you can give a growing reader.
