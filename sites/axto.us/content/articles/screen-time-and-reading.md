Screens are part of every family's life, and many parents worry about what they mean for reading. Will tablets crowd out books? Are e-books as good as print? Do educational apps help children learn to read? The research gives some useful answers, though it is still developing, and the practical lesson is not "screens bad, books good" but something more nuanced: what children do on screens, how much and with whom matters a great deal.

This guide summarizes current guidance, explains how digital and print reading differ, and offers practical ways to protect reading time without turning screens into a battleground.

## What pediatric guidance says

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends:

- **Under 18 to 24 months:** avoid screen media other than video chatting with family.
- **18 to 24 months:** if introducing digital media, choose high-quality programming and watch it together.
- **Ages 2 to 5:** limit screen use to about one hour a day of high-quality programming, ideally co-viewed with a parent who helps the child understand and apply what they see.
- **Ages 6 and older:** set consistent limits on time and types of media, and make sure screens do not displace sleep, physical activity, homework, family time and other healthy activities, such as reading.

The AAP also encourages families to create a family media plan with clear expectations for everyone, including parents.

## Do screens crowd out reading?

The most consistent concern is **displacement**: time spent on screens is time not spent reading, playing, talking or sleeping. Surveys of children's leisure time in many countries have found that reading for pleasure tends to decline as children get older and screen use rises. Screens are not the only reason, but they compete for the same hours, and they are designed to be engaging.

That does not mean screens inevitably destroy reading habits. Families who protect specific times for reading, keep books accessible and model reading themselves can have both.

## Print versus digital reading

### For young children

Studies of parents reading with toddlers and preschoolers have found that with enhanced e-books, those with animations, games and sound effects, parents and children tend to talk less about the story, and children sometimes remember less of it. Interactive features can distract from the narrative and the conversation that makes shared reading so valuable. Simple e-books without many extras are closer to print books in their effects.

### For older children and adults

Research comparing comprehension on screens and on paper, including large meta-analyses, has found a modest advantage for paper, particularly for longer informational texts and when readers are under time pressure. One explanation is that people tend to skim more on screens. The difference is smaller for narrative texts and depends on the device and the reader's habits.

### What this means in practice

E-books and e-readers can be a perfectly good way to read, especially for older children, for travel, for borrowing from the library and for children who need adjustable text size. For young children, print books, or simple e-books read together without distracting features, are the best choice for shared reading.

## What about reading apps?

Many apps promise to teach reading. Some offer useful phonics practice with immediate feedback. Others reward fast tapping more than learning, or teach letter names more than sounds. When evaluating an app, ask:

- Does it teach skills systematically, matching how phonics is taught at school?
- Does it require the child to think, not just tap?
- Are rewards tied to learning, not endless play?
- Is it free of manipulative design, such as constant ads or pressure to buy?
- Can you use it together sometimes?

Apps can supplement, but they cannot replace shared reading, conversation and practice reading real books with a responsive adult.

## Practical family rules that protect reading

### Screen-free reading times

Choose times that are always for books: before bed, during breakfast on weekends, a family reading half-hour after dinner.

### Screens off before bed

Screens in the hour before sleep can interfere with falling asleep. A bedtime story or independent reading in bed is a calmer alternative. See [building a bedtime story routine](/guides/bedtime-story-routine/).

### Keep devices out of bedrooms at night

Charging devices outside bedrooms protects sleep and removes the temptation of late-night scrolling.

### Make books easier to reach than screens

Put books in baskets around the house and keep tablets in a designated place. Ease of access shapes habits.

### Use screens to support reading

Screens can lead to books: a documentary about sharks followed by a library trip for shark books, a film adaptation followed by the novel, a video about a favorite author.

### Model balance

Children notice when adults are always on their phones. Let them see you reading, on paper or on an e-reader, and putting devices away during family time.

## A sample family media plan for reading

| Rule | Example |
|---|---|
| Daily reading time | 20 minutes of reading or being read to, every day |
| Screen-free zones | Bedrooms at night, the dinner table |
| Screen-free times | One hour before bed, family reading time |
| Weekend balance | Screens after a library visit or outdoor time |
| Co-viewing | Watch together and talk about it when possible |
| Devices for reading | E-reader or library app allowed at bedtime only for reading, with blue light reduced |

Adapt the plan to your family's needs and revisit it as children grow.

## Reading on screens well

When children do read on screens, a few habits help them get more from it:

- **Turn off notifications** or use airplane mode, so a message or game alert doesn't interrupt the story.
- **Use a dedicated reading app or e-reader** rather than a general-purpose tablet when possible.
- **Choose simple e-books for young children,** without games or animations that compete with the story.
- **Slow down for informational texts.** Encourage older children to read carefully, take notes or discuss what they read, rather than skimming.
- **Adjust text size and lighting** for comfort, which is one genuine advantage of digital reading for children who find small print tiring.
- **Read together sometimes,** even on a screen, so conversation stays part of reading.

## Screens that lead back to books

Not all screen time competes with reading. Some of it can feed it:

- A nature documentary can spark interest in nonfiction books about animals.
- A film adaptation can motivate a child to read the original book, before or after watching.
- Library apps let children borrow e-books and audiobooks instantly, which helps when a series is in demand.
- Video calls let a grandparent far away read a bedtime story.
- Author videos and online book talks can introduce children to new writers.

The question to ask is not only "how long?" but also "what is this screen time doing for my child?"

## When screens become a battle

If screen limits lead to constant conflict, involve children in creating the rules, explain the reasons, use timers rather than arguments, and offer appealing alternatives: interesting books, outdoor time, board games. Consistency is more important than strictness.

## Myths about screens and reading

**"Any reading on a screen is as good as a book."** For young children, enhanced e-books can distract from the story. For older readers, screens can encourage skimming. Context matters.

**"Educational apps will teach my child to read."** Some help with practice, but children learn to read best through systematic instruction, shared reading and practice with real texts.

**"Screens are always harmful."** Video chatting with grandparents, high-quality programs watched together and library e-books all have value. The concern is displacement and content, not screens as such.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an e-reader better than a tablet for reading?

Dedicated e-readers have fewer distractions than tablets and screens designed for reading, so they are generally better for long reading sessions.

### Should audiobooks count as screen time?

Listening to an audiobook is a different activity from watching videos. Many families treat audiobooks as reading time. See [audiobooks for kids](/guides/audiobooks-for-kids/).

### My child only wants to read on a tablet. Is that a problem?

For an older child reading real books on a tablet, not necessarily. Check that reading is not interrupted by notifications and games, and offer print books too.

## In summary

Follow pediatric guidance on screen time by age, protect specific times for books, prefer print or simple e-books for young children, choose apps carefully and model balance yourself. Screens and reading can coexist when families make deliberate choices about when, how and why screens are used.
