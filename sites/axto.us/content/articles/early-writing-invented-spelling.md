A four-year-old hands you a card covered in wobbly shapes and announces that it says "Happy birthday, Grandma." A kindergartner writes "I LK MI DG" under a drawing and reads it back proudly: "I like my dog." It is tempting to see these efforts as cute mistakes on the way to proper writing. In fact, they are some of the most useful reading practice a young child can do.

Reading and writing are two sides of the same skill. When children read, they turn letters into sounds and words. When they write, they turn the sounds of words into letters. Every attempt to spell a word by its sounds is a small workout for exactly the skills that reading depends on. This guide explains how early writing develops, what "invented spelling" is and why it helps, and how to encourage writing at home without turning it into a chore.

## Reading and writing share the same foundations

To write the word *dog*, a young child has to do several things at once. First, they must hear that the spoken word is made of separate sounds: /d/ /o/ /g/. Then they must remember which letter stands for each sound. Then they must form those letters on paper, from left to right, and remember what they were trying to say in the first place.

Those are the same building blocks that reading uses:

- **Phonemic awareness:** noticing the individual sounds in spoken words. (See [why nursery rhymes matter](/guides/nursery-rhymes-phonological-awareness/) for how this skill begins.)
- **Letter-sound knowledge:** knowing which letters represent which sounds, the heart of [phonics](/guides/what-is-phonics/).
- **Concepts of print:** knowing that writing goes left to right, that words are separated by spaces and that print carries a message. (See [print awareness](/guides/print-awareness-preschool/).)
- **Language:** having something to say and the words to say it.

Teachers sometimes describe spelling as *encoding*, the mirror image of *decoding*. Decoding starts with letters and ends with sounds; encoding starts with sounds and ends with letters. Practicing one strengthens the other, which is why strong early reading programs teach children to spell the same patterns they are learning to read.

## What is invented spelling?

Invented spelling, also called developmental, phonetic or temporary spelling, is a child's attempt to write a word by listening to its sounds and putting down letters for what they hear, before they know the conventional spelling. A child might write *KAT* for cat, *BOT* for boat, *LUV* for love or *SKOOL* for school.

These spellings are not random. They are logical guesses that show exactly what a child can hear and which letters they know. *KAT* tells you that the child heard all three sounds in *cat* and knows a reasonable letter for each one. *BT* for *boat* tells you they can hear the first and last sounds but are not yet hearing the vowel in the middle. For a parent or teacher, a page of invented spelling is a window into a child's growing understanding of how words work.

Research supports the value of this kind of writing. Studies of invented spelling, including experiments by the psychologists Gene Ouellette and Monique Sénéchal, have found that kindergartners who practiced spelling words by their sounds, with gentle feedback, went on to learn to read new words more easily than children who spent the same time on other activities. Trying to spell a word makes a child think hard about its sounds, and that thinking carries over to reading.

## The stages of early writing

Children move through broad stages as their writing develops. The ages below are only rough guides: children vary widely, and many show features of more than one stage at the same time.

1. **Drawing and scribbling (toddlers and young preschoolers).** Children make marks and begin to discover that their marks can mean something. Over time, "writing" scribbles often start to look different from drawing: smaller, in lines, sometimes wavy like cursive.
2. **Letter-like shapes (preschool).** Children produce shapes that resemble letters, mixing real letters, made-up symbols and numbers.
3. **Strings of letters (preschool to early kindergarten).** Children write real letters, often the ones in their own name, but without connecting them to sounds. *AMTOSR* might mean "We went to the park."
4. **First sounds (kindergarten).** Children begin to write a letter for the first sound in a word: *D* for dog, or *I L M D* for "I love my dad."
5. **First and last sounds.** The final sound appears: *DG* for dog, *KT* for cat, *BK* for book.
6. **Phonetic spelling (kindergarten and first grade).** Most sounds are represented, including vowels, though not always conventionally: *KAT*, *SED* for said, *WOK* for walk, *PLAD* for played.
7. **Transitional spelling (first and second grade).** Children use common spelling patterns but sometimes apply them in the wrong place: *TRANE* for train, *JUMPT* for jumped, *HOAP* for hope. These mistakes show that the child is learning patterns, not just sounds.
8. **Conventional spelling.** Most common words and taught patterns are spelled correctly, and children begin to check their own spelling.

Watching for these stages helps you respond to what your child is actually ready for, rather than expecting adult spelling too soon or accepting early spelling for too long.

## Won't invented spelling create bad habits?

This is the most common worry about invented spelling, and it is a fair question. If a child writes *SED* for *said* a hundred times, will that spelling stick?

Invented spelling is a stage, not a destination. It works best alongside systematic phonics teaching, in which children learn letter-sound patterns in a planned order, and alongside plenty of reading, which shows them how words are really spelled. As children learn more patterns and see words repeatedly in books, their spellings move steadily toward the conventional ones.

What matters is that expectations rise as children learn. In preschool and early kindergarten, the goal is simply to get children listening for sounds and putting letters down. By first and second grade, children should be expected to spell correctly the words and patterns they have already been taught, including common irregular words. (See [sight words and heart words](/guides/sight-words-and-heart-words/) for how those tricky words are learned.) Invented spelling for brand-new words remains useful, but it should no longer be the only strategy.

## How to respond to your child's writing

The way adults respond to early writing can make children eager to write more, or anxious about getting it wrong. A few habits help.

- **Ask your child to read it to you.** Children's early writing often makes sense only to them. Asking "Will you read me your story?" shows that you value the message.
- **Celebrate the meaning first.** Respond to what your child wrote about before you look at how it was spelled: "You wrote about your dog! What's his favorite game?"
- **Notice what's right.** Point out specific successes: "You heard the /d/ at the start of *dog* and the /g/ at the end."
- **Offer one small nudge, not ten corrections.** Choose one thing that is just within reach. "Let's say *dog* slowly together: d-o-g. What sound do you hear in the middle?" One gentle step is more useful than a page covered in corrections.
- **Show the "book spelling" without criticism.** Sometimes you can write the conventional spelling underneath and say, "This is how it looks in books." Treat it as interesting information, not as a correction of a failure.

If your child asks you how to spell a word, you can sometimes turn the question back: "What sounds do you hear?" At other times, especially when they are in the middle of a story and want to keep going, just tell them. The aim is to keep writing enjoyable.

## Making writing part of everyday life

Children write more when writing has a real purpose. You do not need worksheets; ordinary family life offers plenty of reasons to write.

- **Lists:** shopping lists, packing lists for a trip, a list of friends to invite to a party.
- **Notes and cards:** birthday cards, thank-you notes, a message on a sticky note for a parent's lunch box.
- **Labels and signs:** labels for drawings, toy boxes or a "Keep out!" sign for a bedroom door.
- **Pretend play:** menus for a pretend restaurant, tickets for a teddy-bear show, prescriptions for a doll at the "doctor's office."
- **Letters to family:** a short letter or postcard to a grandparent, or an email or text written with an adult's help.
- **A family message journal:** a notebook in which you and your child take turns writing short messages to each other.

For children who find holding a pencil tiring, writing can also happen with a finger in sand, with chalk on the sidewalk, with magnetic letters on the fridge or with a paintbrush and water on a fence. These activities still require a child to think about sounds and letters, and they feel like play.

## Handwriting and letter formation

Handwriting is a separate skill from spelling, but the two support each other. Children who can form letters comfortably have more attention left for thinking about sounds and ideas.

Fine motor strength develops gradually. Play with dough, building blocks, beads, scissors and crayons all helps strengthen the small muscles of the hands. A mature pencil grip often develops over the early school years, so there is no need to force one on a preschooler.

When your child is ready to learn letter formation, consistency helps. Many programs teach children to start most letters at the top and to learn lowercase letters early, because most of the print children read is lowercase. Ask your child's teacher which approach their school uses so that home and school match.

Some research, including brain-imaging studies of young children, suggests that forming letters by hand helps children learn to recognize letters better than typing or tracing them does. Typing has its place, especially for older children and for those with handwriting difficulties, but young children benefit from plenty of opportunities to form letters themselves.

## What to expect at different ages

These are broad patterns, not deadlines. For a fuller picture, see [reading milestones by age](/guides/reading-milestones-by-age/).

- **Ages 2–3:** scribbling, beginning to tell drawing apart from writing and "reading" scribbles aloud.
- **Ages 3–4:** letter-like shapes and some real letters, especially from the child's own name.
- **Ages 4–5:** writing their own name, often with some letters reversed; beginning to write the first sounds of words.
- **Ages 5–6:** phonetic spellings that capture more sounds; simple sentences; beginning to leave spaces between words; spelling a few common words correctly.
- **Ages 6–8:** transitional spelling, with correct spelling of many common words and taught patterns, and writing several connected sentences on a topic.

## When to ask for help

Most children's writing develops unevenly, and occasional letter reversals are common until about age seven. Talk to your child's teacher, and consider asking about an evaluation, if you notice:

- persistent difficulty hearing the separate sounds in spoken words, even after teaching;
- very little connection between the letters your child writes and the sounds in words by the middle of first grade;
- spelling that stays far behind reading, or reading and spelling that both lag well behind classmates;
- handwriting that is extremely slow, painful or illegible, or a strong avoidance of any writing.

Difficulties with sounds and spelling can be an early sign of [dyslexia](/guides/dyslexia-explained-for-parents/), and serious handwriting difficulties may need an assessment by an occupational therapist. Early help is far more effective than waiting. See [signs your child may be struggling with reading](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/) and [talking to your child's teacher](/guides/talking-to-your-childs-teacher/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I correct my child's spelling?

Not every time, and never in a way that makes writing feel like a test. For young children, focus on one small, achievable step at a time and celebrate the message. As children move through first and second grade, it becomes more appropriate to expect correct spelling of words they have already been taught, and to help them check their own work.

### My child only wants to type. Is that OK?

Typing is a useful skill, and it can motivate a child who finds handwriting tiring. For young children, though, forming letters by hand seems to help letter learning, so try to keep some handwriting in the mix through drawing, labeling and playful writing. Older children can use both.

### Is it OK if my child reverses letters like b and d?

Yes, in the early years. Many children reverse letters such as b and d, or write their name backwards, as they are learning. Reversals usually fade by around age seven. If they persist beyond that, or come with other reading and spelling difficulties, mention them to the teacher.

### How can I help a child who says "I can't write"?

Lower the stakes. Offer to write part of the message while your child writes one word or one letter. Let them dictate a story to you, then read it back together. Try fun materials such as markers, chalk or a whiteboard. Praise effort and specific successes, and keep sessions short. Confidence grows from small, frequent successes.

## In summary

Early writing and invented spelling are not mistakes to be stamped out; they are powerful practice for reading. When children write words by their sounds, they strengthen the same phonemic awareness and phonics knowledge that reading depends on. Encourage writing for real purposes, respond to the message first, offer one small nudge at a time and raise expectations gradually as your child learns more spelling patterns at school. With patience, today's "I LK MI DG" becomes tomorrow's confident reader and writer.
