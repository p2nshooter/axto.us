Parents naturally wonder whether their child is "on track" with reading. Friends' children seem to read earlier, report cards use unfamiliar terms, and it is hard to know what is typical. This guide describes the reading skills most children develop from kindergarten through third grade in the United States, explains how much variation is normal and suggests what you can do at home at each stage.

A note before the tables: **milestones are typical ranges, not deadlines.** Children develop at different speeds, and a child who is a little behind in one skill may be ahead in another. What matters most is steady progress and catching genuine difficulties early.

## The big picture: learning to read, then reading to learn

A common way to describe early reading is that from kindergarten to around second grade, children are mostly **learning to read**: building phonemic awareness, phonics, word recognition and fluency. From around third grade, the emphasis shifts toward **reading to learn**: using reading to gain knowledge in science, history and other subjects. The shift is gradual, and comprehension matters from the very beginning, but it explains why reading difficulties become more noticeable, and more costly, around third grade.

## Kindergarten (ages 5–6)

| Skill area | Typical by the end of kindergarten |
|---|---|
| Phonological awareness | Recognizes and produces rhymes; claps syllables; identifies the first sound in words; begins to blend and segment simple words |
| Letters and sounds | Names most uppercase and lowercase letters; knows the most common sound for most consonants and short vowels |
| Decoding | Reads simple consonant–vowel–consonant words such as "cat," "sit," "mop" |
| Word recognition | Recognizes a small set of high-frequency words |
| Print concepts | Knows how books work, that print carries meaning and that words are separated by spaces |
| Comprehension | Retells a familiar story; answers simple questions about a read-aloud |
| Writing | Writes their name; writes words with invented spelling that reflects sounds ("kat" for cat) |

**Support at home:** Rhyme and sound games, alphabet play, reading aloud daily and talking about stories, encouraging drawing and writing. See [teaching letter sounds at home](/guides/teaching-letter-sounds-at-home/).

## First grade (ages 6–7)

First grade is often the year of the biggest leap in decoding.

| Skill area | Typical by the end of first grade |
|---|---|
| Phonics | Decodes words with digraphs (sh, ch, th), blends (st, fr), silent e (cake) and common vowel teams (rain, boat) |
| Word recognition | Reads many high-frequency words automatically, including common irregular words |
| Fluency | Reads simple texts aloud fairly smoothly, with growing expression |
| Comprehension | Retells stories with beginning, middle and end; answers who, what, where, why questions; makes simple predictions |
| Vocabulary | Uses new words learned from books and conversation |
| Writing | Writes several sentences on a topic; spells many common words correctly and others phonetically |

**Support at home:** Listen to your child read decodable and simple books daily, help them sound out words rather than guess, keep reading more complex books aloud. See [decodable books](/guides/decodable-books-explained/).

## Second grade (ages 7–8)

| Skill area | Typical by the end of second grade |
|---|---|
| Phonics | Decodes most one-syllable words and many two-syllable words; handles r-controlled vowels (car, bird) and diphthongs (coin, cow) |
| Fluency | Reads grade-level text accurately at a comfortable pace, with expression |
| Comprehension | Understands the main idea; compares characters; uses text features like headings; asks and answers questions about texts |
| Independent reading | Reads short chapter books independently |
| Vocabulary | Uses context and word parts (prefixes, suffixes) to figure out new words |
| Writing | Writes organized paragraphs; uses basic punctuation and capitalization |

**Support at home:** Encourage independent reading of books your child chooses, discuss them together, keep reading aloud at a level above their independent reading. See [early chapter books](/guides/early-chapter-books/).

## Third grade (ages 8–9)

| Skill area | Typical by the end of third grade |
|---|---|
| Decoding | Reads multisyllable words by breaking them into parts |
| Fluency | Reads grade-level text smoothly and with expression, silently and aloud |
| Comprehension | Summarizes; distinguishes main ideas from details; draws inferences; compares texts on the same topic |
| Reading to learn | Gains information from nonfiction texts in science and social studies |
| Vocabulary | Learns new words from reading; understands figurative language like simple idioms |
| Writing | Writes multi-paragraph pieces with a clear structure |

**Support at home:** Offer a wide range of books, including nonfiction and graphic novels, talk about what your child reads and keep reading together. See [why kids need nonfiction too](/guides/nonfiction-for-kids/).

## What screening tells you

Many US schools now screen all young children for early reading risk several times a year, often in kindergarten through third grade. Screeners are short tests, usually a few minutes each, that measure skills such as naming letters quickly, identifying sounds, reading simple nonsense words (to check decoding without memorization) and reading a short passage aloud.

A screening result is not a diagnosis. It flags which children might need closer attention or extra help. If your child's screening shows risk, ask:

- Which skills were below the expected range?
- What extra instruction will my child receive, how often and by whom?
- When will progress be checked again?
- What can I do at home that matches what the school is doing?

Many states now require schools to notify families and provide support when screening identifies a risk of reading difficulties, including dyslexia. Rules vary by state, so it is worth asking your school how it works where you live.

## A year of support at a glance

| Season | Kindergarten | First grade | Second grade | Third grade |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fall | Letter names and sounds, rhymes | Short vowel words, blending | Vowel teams, two-syllable words | Multisyllable words, longer chapter books |
| Winter | First sounds, simple CVC words | Digraphs, blends, silent e | Fluency on grade-level text | Nonfiction for learning, summarizing |
| Spring | Reading simple decodable sentences | Reading short books independently | Short chapter books, main idea | Inference, comparing texts |

Use the table as a rough map, not a checklist. Schools follow different sequences, and your child's teacher can tell you what to expect in your child's class.

## How much variation is normal?

A child who reaches a milestone a few months later than classmates is usually within the normal range, especially in kindergarten and early first grade. Children who started school younger, who are learning English as an additional language or who have had less exposure to books may take a little longer and then catch up quickly with good instruction.

However, "wait and see" can be risky when a child is significantly behind or not making progress. Research consistently shows that reading difficulties are easier to address in kindergarten and first grade than later on.

## Signs worth discussing with the teacher

Talk to your child's teacher if you notice:

- **In kindergarten:** difficulty learning letter names and sounds despite practice, trouble recognizing rhymes or hearing the first sound in words.
- **In first grade:** inability to sound out simple words by the middle of the year, heavy reliance on guessing from pictures, great frustration or avoidance of reading.
- **In second and third grade:** slow, labored reading; difficulty decoding new words; reading accurately but understanding little; strong avoidance of reading.
- **At any age:** a family history of reading difficulties combined with early struggles, or concerns about speech, hearing or vision.

See our guide to [signs your child may be struggling with reading](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/).

## Questions to ask at a parent-teacher conference

- How is my child doing in phonics, fluency and comprehension specifically?
- What screening or assessment results do you have, and what do they mean?
- What is my child working on right now?
- What can I do at home to support this?
- If my child needs extra help, what does the school offer?

## Frequently asked questions

### My child learned to read at four. Does that mean they're gifted?

Early reading is wonderful, but it does not by itself predict later achievement. Keep nurturing vocabulary, knowledge and enjoyment.

### My child is behind but the teacher says not to worry. What should I do?

Ask what specifically the teacher is seeing, what the plan is, and when you will check progress again. If progress stalls, ask about a formal assessment.

### Do milestones differ in other countries?

Yes. Children start formal reading instruction at different ages in different countries. These milestones reflect typical expectations in US schools.

## In summary

Kindergarten builds sound awareness and letter knowledge; first grade is the big decoding year; second grade builds fluency and independence; third grade shifts toward reading to learn. Expect variation, celebrate progress and talk to the teacher early if your child is not moving forward. Your daily read-alouds and conversations support every stage.
