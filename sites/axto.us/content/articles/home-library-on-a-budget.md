Having books at home is one of the most consistent predictors of children's reading success. Large international studies have found that children who grow up with more books at home tend to have stronger literacy skills, even when researchers account for parents' education and income. The good news is that a home library does not have to be expensive. With some planning, families can surround children with books for very little money.

This guide covers where to find free and inexpensive books, how to choose them, and how to organize and rotate your collection so that books actually get read.

## How many books do children need?

There is no magic number. A few dozen well-chosen, regularly rotated books can be more effective than hundreds that sit unread on a high shelf. What matters is that children have **easy access to books that interest them**, that the collection grows with them and that it includes variety: picture books, stories, nonfiction, poetry, comics and books that reflect your family's culture and languages.

## Free and nearly free sources of books

### The public library

The library is the foundation of any budget home library. You can borrow far more books than you could ever buy, try new authors and topics without risk, and keep the home collection fresh. Many libraries let children borrow dozens of books at once and have no late fees for children's materials. Make the library bag part of your home library: a dedicated basket or shelf for current library books keeps them from getting lost.

### Free book programs

Several programs give free books to children:

- **Dolly Parton's Imagination Library** mails a free book every month to children from birth to age five in participating areas of the United States and several other countries.
- **Reach Out and Read** works through pediatric clinics, where doctors give a new book at well-child visits from infancy to age five, along with advice about reading aloud.
- **School and community book giveaways,** such as summer book distributions and literacy events, are common in many areas.

Check with your library, pediatrician and school for programs available where you live.

### Little Free Libraries and book boxes

Neighborhood book-sharing boxes, often called Little Free Libraries, allow anyone to take a book and leave a book. They are a lovely way to find surprises and pass on books your children have outgrown.

### Book swaps

Organize a swap with friends, neighbors, a school or a playgroup. Everyone brings books their children have outgrown and takes home "new" ones. Schools and libraries sometimes host swaps too.

## Inexpensive sources

### Library book sales

Libraries regularly sell donated and withdrawn books for very low prices, often a dollar or less for children's books. Some have ongoing sale shelves.

### Thrift stores and yard sales

Second-hand shops and yard sales frequently have bags of children's books for very little. Check board books for damage and wipe them before use.

### Used book stores and online marketplaces

Used bookstores and online sellers offer classics and popular series at a fraction of new prices.

### School book fairs and book clubs

School book clubs often offer inexpensive paperback editions and bonus points that teachers use to stock classrooms.

## Digital options

Many libraries lend e-books and audiobooks free through apps. Digital borrowing is especially useful for older children who read quickly, for audiobooks on long journeys and when you cannot get to the library. Some public-domain classics are available free online.

## What to include

A balanced home library includes:

- **Board books and picture books** for young children, which are read many times.
- **Read-aloud favorites** that the whole family enjoys.
- **Early readers and decodable books** for children learning to read. See [decodable books](/guides/decodable-books-explained/).
- **Chapter books and series** for independent readers. See [early chapter books](/guides/early-chapter-books/).
- **Nonfiction** on topics your child loves: animals, space, vehicles, sports, how things work. See [why kids need nonfiction too](/guides/nonfiction-for-kids/).
- **Poetry and rhyme,** which build language and are perfect for reading aloud.
- **Comics and graphic novels,** which motivate many reluctant readers.
- **Books in your home language(s)** and books that reflect your family's culture, as well as books about other cultures and experiences.
- **Reference books** like a children's dictionary or atlas.

## Organizing books so they get read

### Keep books low and visible

Put children's books on low shelves or in baskets where children can reach them. Front-facing displays, where covers are visible, are much more inviting than rows of spines.

### Create reading nooks

A cozy corner with cushions, a blanket and good light invites reading. It does not need to be elaborate.

### Rotate the collection

Keep a portion of books stored away and rotate them every few weeks. "New" books generate fresh interest without buying more.

### Organize in a child-friendly way

Sort books in ways your child understands: by topic (animals, trucks, princesses), by type (picture books, chapter books) or by color for very young children. Let children help.

### Spread books around the home

Books in the living room, bedroom, kitchen and car get read far more than books kept in one place.

## A home library that grows with your child

| Age | Core of the collection | Easy budget sources |
|---|---|---|
| 0–2 | Sturdy board books, touch-and-feel, rhymes | Free book programs, thrift stores, hand-me-downs |
| 2–4 | Picture books, first concept books, song books | Library sales, swaps, Little Free Libraries |
| 4–6 | Longer picture books, alphabet books, simple nonfiction | Library, school book clubs |
| 6–8 | Decodable books, early chapter books, first series | Library, used bookstores, series swaps with friends |
| 8–10 | Chapter book series, graphic novels, nonfiction on passions | Library e-books, used series bundles, book fairs |
| 10+ | Novels, graphic novels, reference, magazines | Digital library loans, used bookstores, gifts |

As your child grows, pass on books they have outgrown to younger cousins, neighbors or a Little Free Library, and keep a few beloved favorites as keepsakes.

## Questions to ask your librarian

Librarians are experts at matching children with books, and their advice is free. Useful questions include:

- "My child loves [topic]. What else might they enjoy?"
- "Which series are popular right now for this age?"
- "Do you have decodable books or early readers for a child just learning to read?"
- "Are there free book programs or giveaways in our area?"
- "Can we borrow e-books and audiobooks with our card?"

## Making it personal

- **Write in books** you give as gifts: a date and a message turns a book into a keepsake.
- **Bookplates** with your child's name build ownership.
- **Let children choose** at sales and libraries; ownership of choice builds motivation.
- **Keep a "favorites" shelf** for books your child loves to reread.

## Caring for books on a budget

- Teach gentle handling: turning pages from the corner, keeping food away.
- Repair torn pages with clear tape and loose spines with book tape.
- Clean board books with a slightly damp cloth.
- Pass on books in good condition when your child outgrows them.

## A starter plan for the first three months

| Month | Steps |
|---|---|
| Month 1 | Get library cards for everyone; set up a library basket at home; sign up for any free book program you qualify for |
| Month 2 | Visit a library book sale or thrift store with a small budget; let your child choose; create a reading nook |
| Month 3 | Organize a book swap with friends or school; start rotating books every few weeks |

## Frequently asked questions

### Are library books as good as owned books?

Both are valuable. Library books bring variety; owned books allow rereading and attachment. A mix works best.

### Should I buy the books my child's school recommends?

Check the library first. Buy only books your child will reread or that are hard to borrow.

### What if we live in a small space?

A single basket rotated regularly, combined with frequent library visits, can be enough.

## In summary

A home library is about access and variety, not expense. Use the library, free book programs, swaps, sales and second-hand sources; include picture books, chapter books, nonfiction, poetry, comics and books in your home languages; keep books low, visible and rotated; and let your child choose. A modest, well-used collection can make your home a place where reading happens every day.
