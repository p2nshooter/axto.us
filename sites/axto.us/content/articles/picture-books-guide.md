Picture books are often the first books children fall in love with, and many adults remember particular ones decades later. Yet picture books are sometimes treated as "baby books," to be left behind as soon as a child can read chapter books. That is a loss. The best picture books are sophisticated works of art and literature, and they offer rich language, complex ideas and visual storytelling that benefit readers of every age.

This guide explains what makes picture books special, how to choose great ones, how to read them aloud and why they deserve a place on the shelf well into the elementary years.

## Words and pictures working together

In a picture book, illustrations are not decoration. They are half of the storytelling. The interplay between text and images can work in several ways:

- **Complementary:** words and pictures tell the same story, each adding detail.
- **Expanding:** pictures show what the words leave out, such as a character's expression, a hidden detail or a subplot.
- **Contradicting:** pictures show something different from what the words say, creating humor or irony. A narrator might insist everything is fine while the picture shows chaos.

Children love noticing what the pictures reveal that the words don't say. That noticing is an early form of inference, a key comprehension skill.

## Rich language

Because picture books are read aloud, authors can use vocabulary and sentence structures well beyond what young children could read themselves. Many picture books contain words that rarely appear in everyday speech, such as "glistening," "ferocious" or "meander." Studies comparing the language of picture books with everyday conversation have found that books contain more rare words, which is one reason reading aloud builds vocabulary so effectively.

## What to look for in a great picture book

### A story worth hearing again

Great picture books reward rereading. Look for stories with emotional resonance, humor, surprise or a satisfying ending.

### Language that sounds good aloud

Read a page aloud in the library. Does it have rhythm, interesting words, repetition or rhyme that makes it pleasurable to say?

### Illustrations with depth

Look for art that adds meaning, invites lingering and rewards close looking: expressive characters, details to discover, a distinct style.

### Characters children care about

Children connect with characters who face problems they recognize, whether that's fear of the dark, a new sibling, a lost toy or a big feeling.

### Respect for children

The best picture books don't talk down to children or lecture them. Messages are woven into stories rather than delivered as sermons.

### Diversity and windows and mirrors

Children benefit from books that reflect their own lives (mirrors) and books that show lives different from theirs (windows), an idea associated with education scholar Rudine Sims Bishop. Look for a range of cultures, families, abilities and experiences.

## Types of picture books

| Type | What it offers |
|---|---|
| Story picture books | Narrative, characters, emotional experiences |
| Concept books | Counting, alphabet, colors, opposites |
| Nonfiction picture books | Facts about animals, science, history, biography |
| Wordless books | Visual storytelling; children tell the story in their own words |
| Poetry and rhyme collections | Sound, rhythm, playful language |
| Folk and fairy tales | Cultural traditions, classic story structures |
| Picture book biographies | Real lives told in an engaging, accessible way |

## Reading picture books aloud well

- **Read it yourself first** if you can, so you know where the surprises and page turns are.
- **Show the pictures.** Hold the book so your child can see, and pause to let them look.
- **Use the page turn.** Many picture books build suspense at the page turn. Pause before turning.
- **Use expression and voices,** but don't feel you have to perform.
- **Invite talk.** Ask what your child notices, predicts or feels. See [dialogic reading](/guides/dialogic-reading-peer-method/).
- **Read the endpapers and the back cover.** Illustrators often hide details there.

## Wordless picture books

Wordless picture books tell stories entirely through images. They are valuable for:

- Building oral language: children narrate the story in their own words.
- Developing inference and sequencing.
- Supporting children learning English or families who speak another language at home, since the story can be told in any language.

Ask your child to "read" the story to you, then tell it together in different ways.

## Why older children still need picture books

Many picture books are written for readers aged six to ten or older, not just preschoolers. They offer:

- **Complex themes** handled with brevity and beauty: history, justice, loss, identity, nature.
- **Sophisticated vocabulary and figurative language.**
- **Visual literacy:** analyzing how images create meaning, a skill increasingly important in a visual world.
- **Mentor texts for writing:** picture books are short enough to study how authors craft a story, a strong opening or a satisfying ending.
- **Accessible nonfiction:** picture book biographies and science books introduce topics in depth in a short read.
- **Shared reading across ages:** a picture book can be enjoyed together by a five-year-old and a ten-year-old.

Teachers in upper elementary and even middle school use picture books to introduce topics, model writing and spark discussion.

## Picture books and learning to read

When children are learning to decode, picture books are mostly for reading aloud, because their vocabulary is usually beyond beginning readers. Children practice decoding with [decodable books](/guides/decodable-books-explained/), while picture books build the vocabulary, knowledge and love of stories that make reading meaningful.

## Building a picture book collection

- Borrow widely from the library and buy the books your child returns to again and again.
- Look at award lists for picture books and illustrations as starting points.
- Include classics and new books, fiction and nonfiction, books from many cultures.
- Ask librarians and teachers for favorites. See [building a home library on a budget](/guides/home-library-on-a-budget/).

## A picture book discussion for different ages

The same book can be talked about at very different depths:

| Age | Possible conversation |
|---|---|
| 3–4 | "Where is the cat hiding on this page? How does the boy feel?" |
| 5–6 | "Why do you think he didn't tell his mom? What would you have done?" |
| 7–8 | "The words say he's not scared, but look at his face. What's really going on?" |
| 9–10 | "Why do you think the illustrator used gray colors at the start and bright colors at the end?" |

## Choosing picture books for different purposes

| Purpose | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Bedtime | Calm stories, gentle language, soothing art |
| Building vocabulary | Rich, varied language and interesting words |
| Talking about feelings | Characters facing emotions your child recognizes |
| Learning about the world | Nonfiction picture books and biographies |
| Laughing together | Humor in both the words and the pictures |
| Writing inspiration | Strong openings, repetition, a clear story arc |

## Myths about picture books

**"Picture books are only for little kids."** Many are written for older readers and treat complex subjects.

**"Pictures make reading too easy."** Interpreting how images and words interact is a sophisticated skill.

**"Once they read chapter books, picture books hold them back."** Variety supports growth. Picture books and chapter books build different strengths.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many picture books should we read a day?

As many as you both enjoy. Even one or two a day add up to hundreds a year.

### My child wants the same picture book every night. Should I insist on new ones?

Rereading builds language and comprehension. Offer new books alongside the favorite.

### Are picture books useful for children learning English?

Yes. Illustrations support understanding, and the language is rich and repeatable.

## In summary

Great picture books combine rich language with illustrations that expand and sometimes contradict the words, building vocabulary, inference and visual literacy. Choose books with stories worth rereading, language that sounds good aloud and art with depth. Read them with expression and conversation, try wordless books, and keep picture books in your child's life long after they start reading chapter books.
