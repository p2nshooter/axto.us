Millions of children in the United States grow up speaking a language other than English at home, and many more families want their children to read in two languages. Parents often wonder whether reading in the home language will confuse a child or slow down English reading at school, whether they should switch to English at home, and how to find books in their language.

The research is reassuring. Children can learn to read in two languages, skills built in one language support the other, and a strong home language is an asset. This guide explains how biliteracy develops and offers practical strategies for families.

## Bilingual and biliterate

**Bilingual** usually means speaking and understanding two languages. **Biliterate** means reading and writing in both. Many bilingual children are not biliterate, because they speak the home language but only learn to read in English at school. Biliteracy takes some deliberate support, but it brings advantages: access to family history and culture, connection with relatives, and later academic and career opportunities.

## Myths and facts

**Myth: Learning two languages confuses children.**
Fact: Children around the world grow up with two or more languages. Mixing words from both languages, sometimes called code-switching, is a normal part of bilingual development, not a sign of confusion.

**Myth: Speaking the home language will slow down English reading.**
Fact: Research on cross-linguistic transfer shows that many skills transfer between languages: phonological awareness, the idea that print carries meaning, comprehension strategies and background knowledge. A child who understands stories deeply in Spanish or Vietnamese brings that understanding to English texts.

**Myth: Parents should switch to English to help their children succeed.**
Fact: Speaking with children in the language you know best gives them the richest vocabulary and most complex language model. Switching to a language you speak less fluently can reduce the quality and quantity of conversation.

**Myth: Bilingual children are often late talkers or have language disorders.**
Fact: Bilingual children reach language milestones within the normal range, though their vocabulary may be split across two languages. Language disorders occur at the same rate as in monolingual children and should be evaluated by professionals experienced with bilingual children.

## What transfers between languages

| Skill | Transfers? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Phonological awareness | Yes, largely | Hearing syllables, rhymes and sounds in one language helps in another |
| Concepts of print | Yes | Understanding that print carries meaning, how books work |
| Comprehension strategies | Yes | Predicting, summarizing and inferring work in any language |
| Background knowledge | Yes | Knowledge learned in one language is available in the other |
| Vocabulary | Partly | Concepts transfer; words must be learned in each language, though cognates help |
| Letter-sound correspondences | Partly | Depends on how similar the writing systems are |

Languages that share an alphabet with English, like Spanish, French or Vietnamese, share many letter-sound relationships, though with important differences. Languages with different writing systems, like Arabic, Chinese, Korean or Russian, require learning a separate script, but phonological and comprehension skills still transfer.

## Strategies for families

### Choose an approach that fits your family

Common approaches include:

- **One parent, one language:** each parent consistently uses their language with the child.
- **Home language at home:** the family uses the home language at home, and English at school and in the community.
- **Time or place:** certain times or activities in each language, such as weekends or bedtime stories in the home language.
- **Flexible mixing:** families use both languages naturally.

Consistency helps, but there is no single right approach. What matters most is rich, frequent, enjoyable language in both.

### Read aloud in the home language

Reading aloud in your strongest language builds vocabulary, background knowledge and a love of stories. Translate English picture books on the fly if needed, or tell stories from your own childhood.

### Talk about books in any language

Even if a book is in English, you can discuss it in your home language. Comprehension conversations build understanding regardless of the language of the book.

### Teach reading in the home language

If your home language uses the same alphabet, children may begin to read it naturally once they learn to read English, especially if they have books and encouragement. For languages with different scripts, weekend schools, community classes or online programs can help.

### Connect with community

Community events, cultural centers, places of worship and heritage language schools offer stories, songs and other speakers. Grandparents and relatives can read aloud or tell stories by video call.

### Celebrate both languages

Display books in both languages, label items around the home in both, and talk positively about bilingualism. Children are more motivated to maintain a language they see valued.

## Finding books in other languages

- **Public libraries** often have collections in widely spoken languages and can request others through interlibrary loan.
- **Bilingual books** with text in two languages side by side help families read together when parents and children are stronger in different languages.
- **Digital libraries** offer books in many languages.
- **Relatives abroad** can send books, a meaningful family tradition.
- **Community organizations and consulates** sometimes distribute books.

## Supporting English reading at school

Children learning English at school benefit from:

- Explicit phonics instruction, which helps them map English sounds to letters.
- Rich oral English language development, including vocabulary and conversation.
- Teachers who value and build on their home language.

Share with your child's teacher which languages your child speaks and reads, and how they are developing. Teachers can connect classroom learning to what your child knows.

## Using cognates to boost vocabulary

Many languages share related words with English, called **cognates**: words that look and sound similar and have similar meanings because they come from a common origin. Spanish and English, for example, share thousands: *familia* and family, *animal* and animal, *importante* and important, *biología* and biology.

Pointing out cognates helps bilingual children use what they already know to understand new English words, especially the academic vocabulary of science and social studies, much of which comes from Latin and Greek. A simple game is to find cognates together in a book: "What do you think *enormous* means? It sounds like *enorme*."

Be aware of "false friends," words that look similar but mean something different, such as Spanish *embarazada* (pregnant), which does not mean embarrassed. These are memorable and often funny for children to discover.

## Writing in two languages

Writing supports reading in both languages. Encourage your child to:

- Write letters or messages to relatives in the home language.
- Keep a journal in whichever language they prefer for each entry.
- Make bilingual picture books, with a sentence in each language on every page.
- Label drawings in both languages.

Spelling in each language follows its own rules, and early mistakes that apply one language's spelling to the other are normal and fade with experience.

## When to seek help

If your child has difficulty learning to read in both languages, not just one, or shows signs such as difficulty with rhyming or sounds in the home language too, talk to the school. Assessment should consider both languages, ideally with professionals experienced in bilingual development, to distinguish typical second-language learning from a learning difficulty such as dyslexia. See [signs of reading difficulty](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/).

## Frequently asked questions

### Should my child learn to read in one language first?

Many children learn to read first in the language of school instruction, then transfer skills to the home language. Others learn both at once successfully. Follow your school's program and support the home language at home.

### My child answers in English when I speak our home language. What can I do?

This is common. Keep speaking your language warmly, create situations where the home language is needed, such as calls with relatives, and make it fun with songs, games and stories.

### Will my child's accent in English be affected?

Children who learn English early typically develop native-like pronunciation regardless of the home language.

## In summary

Children can learn to read in two languages, and many skills transfer between them. Speak and read with your child in the language you know best, discuss books in any language, connect with community, find books in both languages and celebrate bilingualism. Biliteracy is a gift that keeps families connected and opens doors for life.
