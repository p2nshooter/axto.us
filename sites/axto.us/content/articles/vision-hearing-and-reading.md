When a child struggles with reading, it's natural to focus on reading skills: letter sounds, decoding, fluency. But before concluding that a child has a reading difficulty, it's important to make sure they can see the page clearly and hear the sounds of language well. Undetected vision or hearing problems can make learning to read harder and can be mistaken for learning difficulties or inattention.

This guide explains why vision and hearing matter for reading, which checks children should have, the warning signs to watch for and what the evidence says about vision and dyslexia.

## Why hearing matters for reading

Learning to read depends on phonological awareness: the ability to hear and work with the sounds in spoken words. A child who doesn't hear speech sounds clearly may struggle to distinguish similar sounds, such as /f/ and /th/ or /b/ and /p/, which can affect phonics learning and spelling.

### Types of hearing problems

- **Temporary hearing loss** from ear infections or fluid in the middle ear (otitis media with effusion) is common in young children. Repeated or prolonged episodes can affect hearing during critical periods of language learning.
- **Permanent hearing loss,** which may be present from birth or develop later, ranges from mild to profound. Even mild hearing loss can affect classroom learning.

### Warning signs of hearing problems

- Frequently saying "What?" or asking for repetition.
- Turning up the volume on devices.
- Not responding when called from another room.
- Speech that is unclear for their age, or delayed speech.
- Difficulty following instructions, especially in noisy places.
- Frequent ear infections or complaints of ear pain.
- Seeming inattentive or daydreaming in class.

## Why vision matters for reading

Reading requires seeing print clearly and comfortably at close range for extended periods. Vision problems can cause blurred print, eye strain, headaches and fatigue, which make reading uncomfortable and may lead children to avoid it.

### Common vision issues in children

- **Refractive errors:** farsightedness (hyperopia), nearsightedness (myopia) and astigmatism, correctable with glasses.
- **Amblyopia ("lazy eye"):** reduced vision in one eye, best treated early.
- **Strabismus:** misalignment of the eyes.
- **Convergence insufficiency:** difficulty keeping the eyes working together at close range, which can cause discomfort and double vision when reading.

### Warning signs of vision problems

- Holding books very close or very far.
- Squinting, rubbing eyes or tilting the head.
- Covering one eye while reading.
- Complaints of headaches, eye strain or blurry or double vision.
- Losing place while reading, skipping lines or using a finger to track (beyond the early stages, when finger-tracking is normal).
- Avoiding reading or close work.

## Recommended checks

### Hearing

Newborns in the United States are routinely screened for hearing at birth. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends hearing screening at regular intervals during childhood, including at several well-child visits from age four onward, and whenever there are concerns about hearing, speech or language. Many schools also screen hearing.

If a screening raises concerns, a full evaluation by an audiologist can determine whether there is hearing loss and what type.

### Vision

Pediatricians check children's eyes at well-child visits, and vision screening is recommended at regular intervals in early childhood. Many schools also screen vision. Screenings detect many problems but can miss some, particularly farsightedness or problems with near vision.

If your child shows warning signs or struggles with reading, a comprehensive eye examination by an optometrist or ophthalmologist can identify problems a screening might miss.

## Vision and dyslexia: what the evidence says

It's important to understand what vision problems do and do not cause:

- **Vision problems can make reading uncomfortable** and should be treated when present.
- **Dyslexia is not caused by vision problems.** It is a language-based difficulty rooted in processing the sounds of language.

The American Academy of Pediatrics, together with ophthalmology organizations, has issued joint statements explaining that dyslexia is a language-based disorder and that there is insufficient scientific evidence to support vision therapy, colored lenses or overlays as treatments for dyslexia. Children with reading difficulties should receive evidence-based reading instruction, while any genuine vision problems are treated separately.

In short: check vision to rule out problems that make reading uncomfortable, but don't expect vision treatment to fix a language-based reading difficulty.

## Other health factors to consider

- **Sleep:** tired children struggle to concentrate and learn.
- **Attention:** attention difficulties, including ADHD, can affect reading and often co-occur with reading difficulties.
- **Speech and language:** underlying language difficulties can affect vocabulary and comprehension. A speech-language pathologist can assess.
- **Emotional wellbeing:** anxiety can affect performance and motivation.

Your pediatrician is a good first contact for any of these concerns.

## A checklist before (or alongside) a reading evaluation

| Check | Who | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Hearing screening or audiology evaluation | Pediatrician, school, audiologist | Rule out hearing loss affecting sound awareness |
| Vision screening or comprehensive eye exam | Pediatrician, school, optometrist, ophthalmologist | Rule out vision problems affecting comfort and clarity |
| Speech and language | Speech-language pathologist | Identify language difficulties affecting reading |
| General health, sleep, attention | Pediatrician | Rule out other factors affecting learning |

Don't delay reading support while waiting for these checks. They can happen in parallel.

## What to do if a problem is found

- **Hearing loss:** follow the audiologist's recommendations, which may include treatment of ear problems, hearing aids or classroom accommodations such as preferential seating or sound systems. Inform the school.
- **Vision problems:** glasses or other treatment as recommended. Make sure your child wears glasses when reading, and let the teacher know.
- **Continued reading difficulty:** if reading struggles persist after vision and hearing are addressed, pursue a reading evaluation. See [signs your child may be struggling with reading](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/) and [reading help options](/guides/reading-help-options/).

## Hearing: signs to watch for at home

Hearing problems can come and go, especially with ear infections and fluid in the middle ear, which are common in young children. Signs that a child might not be hearing clearly include:

- Frequently asking "What?" or needing instructions repeated
- Turning the television or tablet volume up high
- Speaking unusually loudly or softly
- Not responding when called from another room
- Difficulty following conversations in noisy places
- Speech that is unclear for their age, or sounds they consistently mispronounce
- Frequent ear infections or complaints of ear pain

If you notice several of these, ask your child's doctor about a hearing test. Many schools also conduct hearing screenings, but a screening is not a full diagnostic test.

## Vision: signs to watch for at home

Common signs that a child may need a full eye examination include:

- Squinting, rubbing the eyes or covering one eye when reading
- Holding books very close or very far away
- Losing their place often or using a finger to track lines
- Complaints of headaches, blurry vision or tired eyes after reading
- Tilting the head to one side to read
- Avoiding close work or becoming unusually tired after it

School vision screenings typically check distance vision and may miss problems with near focus or eye teaming. If you have concerns, a comprehensive eye examination by an optometrist or ophthalmologist gives a fuller picture.

## Glasses and reading progress

When a child gets glasses for a significant vision problem, reading can become more comfortable, but glasses do not teach a child to read. If a child still struggles with decoding after vision is corrected, the difficulty likely lies elsewhere, and it is important not to wait. For next steps, see [signs of reading difficulty](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/) and [dyslexia explained for parents](/guides/dyslexia-explained-for-parents/).

## Sleep, attention and reading

Tiredness and attention difficulties can look like reading problems. A child who is sleeping poorly may struggle to concentrate, remember and persevere. Conversely, children with attention difficulties may skip words, lose their place or avoid reading. If you notice these patterns, mention them to your child's doctor and teacher so they can be considered alongside any reading assessment.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can glasses fix my child's reading problems?

Glasses correct vision problems and make reading more comfortable, but they don't fix language-based reading difficulties like dyslexia.

### My child passed the school vision screening. Could there still be a problem?

Yes. Screenings can miss some issues, especially farsightedness and near-vision problems. If you notice warning signs, ask for a comprehensive eye exam.

### Do ear infections cause long-term reading problems?

Most children with occasional ear infections develop normally. Frequent or prolonged episodes with hearing loss deserve medical attention and monitoring of speech and language.

## In summary

Before or alongside a reading evaluation, check your child's hearing and vision. Hearing problems can affect sound awareness and phonics; vision problems can make reading uncomfortable. Watch for warning signs, ensure regular screenings and seek full evaluations when concerned. Treat any problems found, but remember that dyslexia is a language-based difficulty that needs evidence-based reading instruction, not vision therapy.
