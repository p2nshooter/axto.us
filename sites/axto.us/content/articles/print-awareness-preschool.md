Before children can read words, they need to understand what print is and how it works. Adults take this knowledge for granted: we know that the black marks on a page are words, that English is read from left to right and top to bottom, and that the words, not the pictures, carry the exact story. Young children have to discover all of this. That discovery is called **print awareness**, or concepts of print.

This guide explains what print awareness includes, why it matters, what to expect at different ages and how to build it naturally during shared reading and everyday life.

## What is print awareness?

Print awareness is a child's understanding of the forms and functions of written language. It includes knowing:

- **Print carries meaning.** The squiggles on the page say something specific, and that message stays the same every time.
- **Books have parts.** A front cover, a back cover, a title, an author, pages.
- **Directionality.** In English, we read from left to right, top to bottom, and turn pages from front to back.
- **Words are separate units.** Spaces separate words, and each written word matches a spoken word.
- **Letters make up words.** Letters are different from words, and there are uppercase and lowercase forms.
- **Punctuation has a purpose.** Periods, question marks and exclamation points change how we read.
- **Print is everywhere.** Signs, labels, menus, lists and screens all contain print with a purpose.

## Why print awareness matters

Research has found that young children's print knowledge is associated with later reading achievement. Children who understand how print works can focus on learning letter-sound relationships when formal instruction begins, instead of puzzling over which way to read a line or what counts as a word.

Print awareness is also part of becoming motivated to read. A child who understands that print holds stories, instructions and messages has a reason to want to unlock it.

## Milestones by age

Children develop print awareness gradually, mostly through exposure to books and print in daily life.

| Age | Typical print awareness |
|---|---|
| 2–3 | Holds a book the right way up, turns pages (often several at once), recognizes favorite book covers, knows logos and familiar signs |
| 3–4 | Understands that print, not pictures, tells the words; "pretend reads" familiar books; recognizes the first letter of their name |
| 4–5 | Knows where a book starts and the direction to read; recognizes some letters; understands that words are separated by spaces; writes or "writes" their name |
| 5–6 | Points to words one by one while reading a memorized text; distinguishes letters, words and sentences; notices punctuation; recognizes some common words |

These are general guides. Children who have had fewer experiences with books catch up quickly when given those experiences.

## Building print awareness during shared reading

The good news is that you do not need to teach print awareness formally. Small additions to ordinary read-alouds make a big difference. Studies of "print referencing," where adults draw children's attention to print while reading, have found improvements in children's print knowledge.

### Talk about the book itself

- "This is the front cover. Here's the title: *The Snowy Day*."
- "The author is the person who wrote the words. The illustrator drew the pictures."
- "Can you show me where the story starts?"

### Point to the words

Occasionally run your finger under the words as you read, especially on short, repeated lines. This shows that you read the print, left to right, and that each written word matches a spoken word.

### Notice letters and words

- "Look, this word starts with B, like your name, Ben!"
- "This word is really long. This one is short."
- "There's the word 'stop' again!"

### Highlight special print

Many picture books have words in big letters, speech bubbles or sound effects like "CRASH!" These are perfect for pointing out: "This word is big because the giant is shouting."

### Talk about punctuation

- "This mark is a question mark. It means someone is asking something."
- "The exclamation mark tells us to read it with excitement!"

### Keep it light

One or two print comments per book is plenty. Story enjoyment comes first. If every page becomes a lesson, reading stops being fun.

## Building print awareness in daily life

Print is everywhere, and everyday moments are full of opportunities:

- **Signs and labels.** Point out stop signs, store names, street names and labels on food.
- **Lists.** Write a shopping list together and let your child "read" it at the store.
- **Recipes.** Follow a simple recipe, pointing to steps as you go.
- **Mail and messages.** Read birthday cards, letters and notes aloud.
- **Name recognition.** Label your child's belongings and artwork with their name.
- **Environmental print.** Cereal boxes, menus and logos are often a child's first "words."

## Writing and drawing

Early writing supports print awareness powerfully. Provide crayons, markers and paper, and encourage scribbling, drawing and "writing."

- Accept all writing attempts. Scribbles and letter-like shapes are steps toward writing.
- Write down your child's words. Ask your child to tell you about a drawing and write their words under it, reading them back.
- Help your child write their name. It is often the first word children learn to write and read.
- Make simple books. Fold paper into a booklet, let your child draw pictures and write their story for them.

## Letter knowledge

Knowing letter names and shapes is closely related to print awareness and is one of the strongest early predictors of reading success. You can build it through alphabet books, magnetic letters, letter puzzles and noticing letters in everyday print. Focus first on letters that matter to your child, like those in their name.

## Signs your child may need more support

By the end of kindergarten, most children understand basic concepts of print. Talk to your child's teacher if your child:

- Shows little interest in books or print by age four to five, despite regular exposure.
- Does not know which way to hold a book or where to start reading by age five.
- Has trouble recognizing letters in their own name by age five.

Often, more exposure is all that is needed. Sometimes, difficulty with print awareness is one sign among several of a learning difference, and early support helps. See our guide to [signs of reading difficulty](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/).

## A five-minute print walk

Here is a simple activity you can do in any neighborhood, supermarket or library. Give your child a "mission" to find print with a purpose:

- A sign that tells people to stop or go.
- A word on a shop that tells us what is sold inside.
- A number on a house or a bus.
- A label that tells us what is in a box or a can.
- A letter from your child's name.

Each time your child finds one, talk about what the print does: "That sign tells drivers to stop so people can cross safely." This turns abstract ideas about print into something children see and use. Repeat it on different outings, and let your child take a photo of favorite finds to "read" again at home.

## Common myths

**"Print awareness means teaching my preschooler to read."** It doesn't. It means helping children understand what print is and how it works, which prepares them for reading instruction later.

**"Children pick it up automatically."** Many do, but only when they have had lots of experiences with books and print. Drawing children's attention to print, even briefly, speeds the process.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I point to every word as I read?

No. Pointing occasionally, especially on short or repeated text, is effective. Pointing constantly can distract from the story.

### Is it okay that my child "reads" by memorizing?

Yes. Pretend reading of memorized books is an important stage. It shows your child understands that books tell stories in a fixed form.

### Do digital books help print awareness?

Print awareness can develop with digital books too, but features like animations and games can distract from the text. Reading together and talking about the words matters most.

## In summary

Print awareness is the understanding of what print is and how it works: that it carries meaning, has a direction and is made of words and letters. Build it by talking about books, pointing out words and letters occasionally, noticing print in daily life and encouraging early writing. These small moments prepare children to make sense of formal reading instruction when it begins.
