Dyslexia is one of the most common learning differences, and one of the most misunderstood. Many people believe it means seeing letters backwards, that it reflects low intelligence or that children will grow out of it. None of these is accurate. Understanding what dyslexia really is helps parents recognize it, get the right help and support their child's confidence.

This guide explains dyslexia in plain language: what it is, how common it is, the signs, how it's identified, what effective help looks like and how to support your child.

## What dyslexia is

The International Dyslexia Association describes dyslexia as a specific learning disability that is neurobiological in origin. It is characterized by difficulties with accurate and/or fluent word recognition and by poor spelling and decoding abilities. These difficulties typically result from a deficit in the phonological component of language, the ability to process the sounds of spoken words, and are often unexpected in relation to a person's other abilities and the instruction they have received.

In plain terms: **dyslexia makes it hard to connect letters with the sounds they represent,** which makes decoding words and spelling difficult. It is not a problem with vision, intelligence or effort.

## What dyslexia is not

- **Not seeing letters backwards.** Many young children reverse letters while learning; this is common and not a sign of dyslexia on its own.
- **Not a lack of intelligence.** Dyslexia occurs across the full range of intelligence. Many people with dyslexia are highly capable thinkers.
- **Not laziness.** Children with dyslexia often work much harder than their peers to read.
- **Not caused by poor teaching or parenting,** although quality of instruction affects how well a child with dyslexia learns to read.
- **Not something children outgrow.** Dyslexia is lifelong, but with effective instruction people with dyslexia learn to read and succeed.

## How common is dyslexia?

Estimates vary depending on how dyslexia is defined. Commonly cited figures suggest that roughly 5 to 10 percent of people have dyslexia, and the International Dyslexia Association notes that as many as 15 to 20 percent of the population may have some symptoms. Dyslexia often runs in families: a child with a parent or sibling who has dyslexia is at higher risk.

## Signs of dyslexia at different ages

### Preschool

- Difficulty learning nursery rhymes or recognizing rhyming words.
- Trouble learning letter names.
- Persistent mispronunciation of words.
- A family history of reading difficulties.

### Kindergarten and first grade

- Difficulty connecting letters and sounds.
- Trouble blending sounds into words or breaking words into sounds.
- Reading errors unrelated to the letters on the page.
- Difficulty reading simple words.
- Frustration or avoidance of reading.

### Second grade and beyond

- Slow, laborious reading.
- Difficulty decoding unfamiliar words, including multisyllable words.
- Very weak, inconsistent spelling.
- Avoiding reading aloud.
- Strong listening comprehension and verbal skills that contrast with weak reading.
- Difficulty learning a foreign language.

## Strengths often seen alongside dyslexia

Many people with dyslexia have strengths in areas such as big-picture thinking, problem-solving, creativity, spatial reasoning, storytelling and verbal expression. These strengths vary from person to person and should not be assumed, but recognizing them matters for a child's confidence.

## How dyslexia is identified

Identification usually involves a comprehensive evaluation that looks at:

- Phonological awareness and phonological processing.
- Decoding of real words and nonsense words.
- Reading fluency and comprehension.
- Spelling and writing.
- Rapid naming (how quickly a child can name familiar items like letters or colors).
- Oral language and vocabulary.
- Cognitive abilities and other factors, such as attention.

Evaluations can be done by schools through the special education process or privately by qualified professionals such as educational psychologists or neuropsychologists. Schools sometimes use terms like "specific learning disability in basic reading skills" rather than "dyslexia," though many states now encourage or require schools to use the word dyslexia.

Many US states have dyslexia laws requiring early screening and support. Ask your school how screening works in your state.

## What effective help looks like

Research supports **structured literacy** for children with dyslexia: instruction that is:

- **Explicit:** skills are directly taught, not left to discovery.
- **Systematic and cumulative:** skills follow a logical order, from simple to complex, with constant review.
- **Diagnostic:** teaching is adjusted based on ongoing assessment of the child's progress.
- **Focused on phonology, phonics and morphology,** as well as fluency, vocabulary and comprehension.
- **Often multisensory:** engaging sight, sound and movement, such as tracing letters while saying sounds.

Programs based on Orton-Gillingham principles are widely used examples. Effective intervention is usually delivered frequently, in small groups or one-to-one, by trained teachers.

## Accommodations

Alongside instruction, accommodations help children access learning while reading skills develop:

- Extra time on tests and assignments.
- Audiobooks and text-to-speech for content subjects. See [audiobooks for kids](/guides/audiobooks-for-kids/).
- Reduced reading load or alternative ways to show knowledge.
- Not being required to read aloud in front of the class without preparation.
- Assistive technology for writing, such as speech-to-text and spell-checkers.

Accommodations do not replace instruction; they make sure dyslexia doesn't block learning in other subjects.

## Supporting your child at home

### Keep reading aloud

Read-alouds and audiobooks keep vocabulary, knowledge and love of stories growing while decoding skills develop.

### Practice what the specialist recommends

Short, positive, consistent practice of skills from the intervention program is valuable. Ask the teacher or tutor what to do at home.

### Protect confidence

- Explain dyslexia honestly in age-appropriate terms: "Your brain learns reading in a different way, and you're learning the skills that help."
- Praise effort and progress.
- Highlight strengths and interests outside reading.
- Share examples of successful people with dyslexia, while being realistic.

### Work with the school

Stay in close contact with teachers, ask about progress monitoring, and participate in meetings about support plans. See [reading help options](/guides/reading-help-options/).

### Take care of yourself

Supporting a child with dyslexia can be demanding. Connect with other parents through local or national dyslexia organizations.

## A day in the life: supporting dyslexia without overload

| Time | Support |
|---|---|
| Morning | Brief review of a few sounds or words from the intervention program, five minutes |
| School | Structured literacy intervention; accommodations such as audiobooks in science |
| After school | Downtime and activities your child excels at |
| Evening | Short decodable reading practice with praise for effort |
| Bedtime | You read aloud a book your child loves, purely for enjoyment |

## Myths about dyslexia

**"Dyslexia can't be identified until third grade."** Signs can be noticed in preschool and kindergarten, and early screening and intervention are encouraged.

**"Colored overlays or special fonts cure dyslexia."** There is limited evidence that these help reading for most people with dyslexia. Structured literacy instruction is the core of effective help.

**"Kids with dyslexia can't learn to read."** With effective instruction, people with dyslexia learn to read, though it may take more time and practice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is dyslexia genetic?

It tends to run in families, and research indicates a strong genetic component.

### Can dyslexia be cured?

Dyslexia is lifelong, but effective instruction helps people with dyslexia become capable readers and writers.

### Should I tell my child they have dyslexia?

Many experts recommend explaining it honestly and positively, so the child understands their difficulties are not their fault.

## In summary

Dyslexia is a common, lifelong, neurobiological difference that makes connecting letters and sounds difficult, affecting decoding, fluency and spelling. It is not about intelligence, effort or seeing letters backwards. Watch for early signs, seek evaluation, ask for structured literacy instruction and appropriate accommodations, keep reading aloud and protect your child's confidence. With the right help, children with dyslexia learn to read and thrive.
