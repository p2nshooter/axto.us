A letter comes home from school. Your child has taken a reading screener, and the results say "Below Benchmark" in one area and give a percentile in another. There is a chart with colored bands. Perhaps it mentions "nonsense word fluency," which sounds alarming. What does it all mean, and what should you do next?

Reading screeners are now a routine part of the early school years in many places, and they can be extremely useful. They help schools identify children who need extra help with reading early, when that help is most effective. But the reports can be confusing, and a single score is easy to misread. This guide explains what screeners are, what they measure, how to understand the scores and which questions to ask.

## What a reading screener is

A reading screener is a short assessment given to all students in a grade, often called universal screening, to find children who may be at risk of reading difficulties. In the early grades it is usually given three times a year, in the fall, winter and spring, so that schools can see who is on track and who needs more support.

Screeners are quick. In kindergarten and first grade, many are given one-on-one, with each task taking only a minute or two. Later, some are taken on a computer.

A good way to think of a screener is like a hearing check at a pediatrician's office. It is not designed to diagnose a condition or describe everything about a child. Its job is to flag children who may need a closer look, so that help can start early.

Schools use a range of tools. Some common ones include DIBELS (Dynamic Indicators of Basic Early Literacy Skills), Acadience Reading and aimswebPlus, which use short timed tasks, and computer-adaptive assessments such as i-Ready, MAP Growth and Star, in which the questions get harder or easier depending on a child's answers. Many states require or recommend particular screeners, and some require screening for characteristics of dyslexia.

## What screeners measure, and why some tasks look odd

Early reading screeners usually measure the specific skills that research has shown predict later reading success. Depending on the tool and the grade, these may include:

- **Letter naming:** how many letters a child can name in a short time. Fast, accurate letter knowledge in kindergarten is a strong predictor of later reading.
- **Phonemic segmentation:** whether a child can break a spoken word into its individual sounds, such as saying /m/ /a/ /p/ for *map*.
- **Nonsense word reading:** reading made-up words such as *vab*, *tig* or *lom*.
- **Word reading:** reading real words from a list.
- **Oral reading fluency:** reading a short passage aloud for one minute. The score is usually the number of words read correctly, along with accuracy.
- **Comprehension tasks:** for example, a "maze" task in which a child reads a passage silently and chooses the correct word from three options at regular points.

Nonsense words often worry parents, but they have a clear purpose. A child cannot have memorized a made-up word, so reading one correctly shows that the child can actually decode, using letter-sound knowledge to work out an unfamiliar word. It is a clean test of phonics skills.

Many tasks are timed because speed reflects how automatic a skill has become. A child who knows letter sounds well enough to use them instantly has more attention left for understanding. Accuracy matters as well, and a good report shows both.

## Understanding the scores

### Benchmark goals and risk categories

Most screeners compare a child's score with a benchmark goal, a research-based cut point for that grade and time of year. Children who score at or above the benchmark are likely to be on track to reach later reading goals with good classroom instruction. Many tools sort results into categories such as "well below benchmark," "below benchmark," "at benchmark" and "above benchmark," often shown in colors.

A result below benchmark means a child may need extra support in that skill. It does not mean that the child has a learning disability, and it does not predict the future with certainty. It is a signal to look more closely and to act.

### Percentiles

Some reports give a percentile, which compares your child's score with a large group of students in the same grade. A score at the 40th percentile means your child scored higher than about 40 percent of students in that comparison group. A percentile is not the percentage of questions answered correctly. Small differences in percentiles, especially in the middle of the range, are usually not meaningful.

### Composite scores

Some tools combine several measures into a single composite score that gives an overall picture. It is useful for a quick summary, but always look at the individual skills as well: a composite can hide a specific weakness, such as decoding, that needs attention.

### Growth over time

A single score is a snapshot taken on one day. Children have good and bad days, and a score can be affected by tiredness, illness, nerves or an unfamiliar tester. The pattern across fall, winter and spring, and how much a child grows, often tells you more than any single number.

## What happens after a screening

Many schools organize reading support through a framework called a Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS), sometimes known as Response to Intervention (RTI). It usually has three tiers:

- **Tier 1:** strong core reading instruction for every student in the classroom.
- **Tier 2:** extra small-group instruction for students who need more help with particular skills.
- **Tier 3:** more intensive, often individual, support for students who need it most.

If a screener flags a possible risk, the school may give a diagnostic assessment to find out exactly which skills need work. For example, a phonics survey might show that a child knows short vowels but not consonant blends.

Children receiving extra help are usually checked with brief progress monitoring, often every week or two, to see whether the support is working. Results are often plotted on a graph against a goal line, so that teachers can see quickly whether a child is catching up or whether the plan needs to change.

If a child does not make enough progress despite good support, the school may recommend an evaluation for special education services. In the United States, parents can request an evaluation in writing at any time, and the school must respond. ## Questions to ask about your child's results

When you receive a screening report, or at a parent-teacher meeting, these questions can help:

- Which screener was used, and which skills did it measure?
- What were my child's scores, and what was the benchmark goal for each skill?
- Which skills are strengths, and which need more work?
- What extra support will my child receive? Which program, how many minutes a week, in what group size and with which teacher?
- How will progress be monitored, and when can we look at the results together?
- What can we do at home that matches what my child is learning at school?
- When will we meet again to review whether the plan is working?

Write down the answers, and ask for copies of the reports so that you can track progress over time. For more on these conversations, see [talking to your child's teacher about reading concerns](/guides/talking-to-your-childs-teacher/).

## What screeners can't tell you

Screeners are useful, but they have limits.

- **They are not a diagnosis.** A screener can flag a risk of reading difficulties, including dyslexia, but it cannot diagnose dyslexia or any other condition.
- **They do not measure intelligence or potential.** A child who scores below benchmark is not less capable; they need more support with particular skills.
- **They give a narrow picture.** Early screeners focus on foundational skills such as letters, sounds and decoding. They say less about vocabulary, background knowledge, motivation and deeper comprehension. A child with strong decoding but weaker language comprehension may pass early screeners and struggle later, when texts become more complex. (See [the science of reading](/guides/science-of-reading-overview/).)
- **Language matters.** For children who are learning English, scores may partly reflect their stage of English learning rather than their reading ability. Schools should interpret results carefully and, where possible, consider skills in the home language. (See [raising bilingual readers](/guides/raising-bilingual-readers/).)
- **Testing conditions matter.** Anxiety, illness or a noisy room can affect results. If a score seems surprising, ask whether it can be checked.

## How to help at home without "teaching to the test"

It can be tempting to practice the screening tasks at home, but drilling nonsense words or timing your child with a stopwatch is unlikely to help and may create anxiety. Instead, support the underlying skills in ways that feel natural:

- play sound games, such as finding things that start with the same sound or clapping the syllables in names;
- practice letter sounds with magnetic letters, [letter-sound games](/guides/teaching-letter-sounds-at-home/) and alphabet books;
- read [decodable books](/guides/decodable-books-explained/) that match the phonics your child is learning at school;
- reread favorite books and short passages to build fluency (see [why rereading favorites helps](/guides/rereading-favorite-books/));
- keep reading aloud and talking about books to grow vocabulary and comprehension.

These activities build real reading skills, which is exactly what screeners are trying to measure.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why is my child being timed? Doesn't that cause stress?

Timed tasks measure how automatic a skill has become, which is an important part of reading. Trained testers present the tasks calmly, often as games, and most young children do not find them stressful. If your child is very anxious about testing, mention it to the teacher so that it can be taken into account.

### My child scored "above benchmark" in kindergarten but is struggling now. How?

Early screeners focus on foundational skills such as letter knowledge and decoding. As texts get longer and more complex, reading depends more on vocabulary, background knowledge and comprehension, and on keeping up with more advanced phonics patterns. A child can be strong in early skills and still need help later. Ask the teacher which skills are causing difficulty now.

### Can I see the actual test?

Schools may not be able to share test materials, but you can ask to see your child's scores, the benchmark goals and examples of the kinds of tasks used. Many screener publishers provide family-friendly explanations of what each task measures.

### Should I prepare my child for the screener?

No special preparation is needed, and practicing the specific tasks is not recommended. The best preparation is ordinary reading support at home: reading aloud, playing with sounds, practicing letter sounds and reading books at the right level together. Make sure your child is well rested on screening days.

## In summary

Reading screeners are quick checks that help schools find children who need extra reading support early. They measure skills such as letter naming, phonemic awareness, decoding and fluency, and they compare scores with benchmark goals. A result below benchmark is a signal to look more closely and to provide help, not a diagnosis. Ask which skills were measured, what support will follow and how progress will be monitored, and support the underlying skills at home through reading, talking and play rather than test practice.
