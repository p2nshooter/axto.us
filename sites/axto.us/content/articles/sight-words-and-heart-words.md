Many children bring home lists of "sight words" in kindergarten and first grade: the, said, was, you, of, they. Parents are often asked to practice them with flashcards until the child can say each word instantly. At the same time, schools increasingly talk about phonics and "heart words." It is easy to feel confused about what your child is supposed to be learning and how.

This guide untangles the terms, explains what research says about how children learn to recognize words instantly and shows a practical way to help your child master tricky words at home.

## Three terms that are often mixed up

### High-frequency words

These are the words that appear most often in written English. A small number of words make up a large share of everything we read: "the," "and," "a," "to," "in," "is," "you," "that" and so on. Lists such as the Dolch list (compiled in the 1930s and 1940s) and the Fry list (based on later research) collect these words. Knowing them well helps children read fluently, because they appear in almost every sentence.

### Irregular or "tricky" words

Some high-frequency words are spelled in ways that do not follow the phonics patterns children have learned yet. "Said" does not sound like "s-a-i-d" would suggest. "Of" ends in /v/ even though it is spelled with an f. "Was" sounds like "wuz." These words cannot be fully sounded out with early phonics knowledge.

Importantly, **many high-frequency words are perfectly regular**: "and," "it," "in," "can," "but," "not," "big." Children can decode them with basic phonics.

### Sight words

Here the terminology gets slippery. In everyday school use, "sight words" often means a list of high-frequency words to memorize. In reading research, a **sight word** is any word a reader recognizes instantly and effortlessly, without needing to sound it out. By that definition, the goal is for *every* familiar word to become a sight word eventually, whether it is regular like "cat" or irregular like "said."

## How words become instantly recognizable

Research on reading, especially the work of Linnea Ehri on **orthographic mapping**, suggests that children store words in memory by connecting the letters in a word to the sounds in its pronunciation. When a child decodes "ship" as /sh/ /i/ /p/, the brain links those letters to those sounds. After a few successful decodings, the word is stored and can be retrieved instantly.

This means that instant recognition is built *through* phonics, not separately from it. Children who try to memorize words as visual shapes, without attending to the letters and sounds, tend to struggle as the number of words grows. Many words look similar ("was," "saw," "won"), and the visual memory approach eventually breaks down.

## Why memorizing by shape is not ideal

Traditional flashcard drills sometimes encourage children to recognize words by their overall shape or a single feature, such as a tall letter or the first letter. This can work for a handful of words but causes problems later:

- Children confuse visually similar words.
- They develop a habit of guessing rather than looking at all the letters.
- They do not build the letter-sound connections that make words stick.

That does not mean high-frequency word practice is useless. It means *how* the words are taught matters.

## The heart word method

A widely used alternative is the **heart word** approach. It treats tricky words as mostly decodable with one or two parts that must be "learned by heart."

Here is how it works with the word "said":

1. **Say the word** and use it in a sentence: "She said hello."
2. **Count the sounds:** /s/ /e/ /d/. Three sounds.
3. **Map the sounds to letters:** s = /s/, ai = /e/, d = /d/.
4. **Find the tricky part:** "ai" making the /e/ sound is unusual. Draw a heart above it; that is the part to learn by heart.
5. **Notice the regular parts:** the s and d are exactly what we expect.
6. **Practice:** read it, spell it aloud, write it, use it in sentences.

This approach keeps children looking at every letter and connecting it to sound. Only the truly irregular part needs memorization.

### Temporary and permanent heart words

Some words are only "heart words" for a while. "Like" seems tricky until children learn the silent e pattern, and then it becomes fully decodable. Other words, such as "of" and "said," stay irregular. Teachers often call these temporary and permanent heart words.

## Practicing high-frequency words at home

### Go from sound to print

Say the word, tap out its sounds, then look at how each sound is spelled. This mirrors how the word will be stored.

### Spell as well as read

Spelling a word reinforces memory for its letters. Have your child write the word while saying each sound.

### Use the words in real reading

Words stick best when children meet them in books. Decodable texts and simple stories give lots of practice in context.

### Keep it short and varied

A few words at a time, practiced for a few minutes in several ways, works better than a long list drilled at once.

### Games that help

- **Word hunt:** find the week's words in a picture book.
- **Build it:** make the word with magnetic letters, then mix the letters up and rebuild it.
- **Sentence challenge:** make a silly sentence using as many of the words as possible.
- **Read and race:** read a short list against a timer, aiming to beat yesterday's time, only once the words are already familiar.

## How many words should my child know?

Schools often set targets, such as recognizing a certain number of high-frequency words by the end of kindergarten or first grade. Targets vary by district and program. More useful than a count is whether your child:

- Decodes simple regular words accurately.
- Recognizes common irregular words quickly in text.
- Spells high-frequency words with increasing accuracy.
- Reads connected text with growing fluency.

## When to be concerned

Some children find it unusually hard to remember words, even after many repetitions, or keep confusing words like "was" and "saw" well into first grade. If your child is working hard but not retaining words, or relies heavily on guessing, talk to the teacher. Difficulty storing words in memory can be a sign of weak phonological skills or dyslexia, and targeted help is effective. See [signs of reading difficulty](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/).

## A one-week heart word plan

Here is a simple way to work through three new tricky words in a week, in about five minutes a day. Suppose the words are "said," "was" and "they."

| Day | Activity | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Introduce "said": say it, use it in a sentence, tap the sounds, map them to letters, draw a heart over "ai." Read it in two sentences. | 5 minutes |
| Tuesday | Review "said." Introduce "was" the same way (heart over "a" saying /u/ and "s" saying /z/). Spell both aloud. | 5 minutes |
| Wednesday | Review both. Introduce "they" (heart over "ey" saying /ay/). Build all three with magnetic letters. | 5 minutes |
| Thursday | Word hunt: find the three words in a favorite book. Write each one while saying the sounds. | 5 minutes |
| Friday | Silly sentences: "They said the cat was a dog!" Read them, then write one from dictation. | 5 minutes |

Keep old words in a review pile and mix a few into each session. Words that are read and spelled correctly on several different days can "graduate."

## Myths about sight words

**"Some children are visual learners, so they should memorize words by shape."** Research on learning styles has not found that matching teaching to a preferred style improves learning, and word recognition in particular depends on linking letters to sounds for every reader.

**"If my child knows the list, they can read."** Knowing a list of common words helps fluency, but reading new words depends on decoding. A child who can only recognize memorized words will struggle when books become more varied.

**"Phonics doesn't work for high-frequency words."** Most high-frequency words are fully or largely decodable. Only small parts of a few words need to be learned by heart.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I still use the sight word list from school?

Yes, but practice the words by connecting letters to sounds rather than by shape alone. Ask the teacher whether the school uses heart word methods.

### Are flashcards bad?

Not necessarily. Brief flashcard review can help with speed once a word has been learned through its sounds. The problem is using flashcards as the only method.

### What about very common words like "the"?

"The" is so common that children learn it quickly. Point out that the t and h together make /th/, and the e makes a short "uh" sound here, which is the heart part.

## In summary

High-frequency words are common words; some are regular and some are tricky. Instant recognition of any word, called a sight word in research, is built by connecting letters to sounds through orthographic mapping. Teach tricky words with the heart word method, focusing on the irregular part, and practice by reading, spelling and meeting words in real books. That way, word lists support phonics instead of competing with it.
