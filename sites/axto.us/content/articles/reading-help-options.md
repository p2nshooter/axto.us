When a child struggles with reading, parents are suddenly faced with a maze of options and acronyms: intervention, MTSS, RTI, IEP, 504 plan, structured literacy, tutoring. Knowing what each option is, who it is for and how to access it helps you advocate effectively for your child.

This guide explains the main types of reading support available through US public schools and outside school, and how to decide what your child needs. Laws and procedures vary by state, so use this as an orientation and check the details with your school and state education agency.

## Layers of support in school

Many schools organize support in tiers, often called a **Multi-Tiered System of Supports (MTSS)** or **Response to Intervention (RTI)**.

| Tier | What it is | Who gets it |
|---|---|---|
| Tier 1 | High-quality core reading instruction in the classroom | All students |
| Tier 2 | Targeted small-group intervention, several times a week | Students who are below benchmark on screening |
| Tier 3 | More intensive, often smaller-group or individual intervention | Students who don't respond enough to Tier 2 |

Progress is monitored frequently, and students move between tiers based on how they respond. MTSS can be a fast way to get help without waiting for a formal evaluation.

**Important:** Under federal law, schools cannot use MTSS or RTI to delay or deny an evaluation for special education when a disability is suspected. Parents can request an evaluation at any time.

## Classroom accommodations and supports

Even without a formal plan, teachers can provide support:

- Seating near the teacher, extra time or reduced reading load.
- Audiobooks or text-to-speech for content subjects.
- Preview of texts before class discussions.
- Not requiring reading aloud without preparation.

Ask the teacher what informal supports are possible.

## Special education evaluation

If you suspect your child has a disability affecting reading, such as dyslexia, you can request an evaluation under the **Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA)**.

### How to request an evaluation

1. **Write a letter or email** to the school principal or special education coordinator stating that you suspect a disability and requesting a comprehensive evaluation. Date it and keep a copy.
2. **Describe your concerns** briefly: specific reading difficulties, family history, lack of progress with intervention.
3. **The school must respond.** If it agrees, it will ask for your written consent to evaluate.
4. **The evaluation must be completed within a set timeline** after consent: generally 60 days under federal law, or a different timeline set by your state.
5. **If the school declines,** it must give you written notice explaining why. You can challenge the decision through procedures described in your procedural safeguards notice.

### What the evaluation includes

A comprehensive evaluation looks at reading skills (phonological awareness, decoding, fluency, comprehension), spelling and writing, oral language, cognitive abilities and other relevant areas. It may involve school psychologists, special education teachers and speech-language pathologists.

### Independent evaluations

If you disagree with the school's evaluation, you may have the right to request an Independent Educational Evaluation (IEE) at public expense. You can also obtain a private evaluation and share it with the school, which must consider it.

## The IEP

If your child is found eligible for special education, for example under the category of "specific learning disability," the school develops an **Individualized Education Program (IEP)** with you.

An IEP includes:

- Your child's present levels of performance.
- Measurable annual goals.
- Specially designed instruction, such as structured literacy intervention, including how often, how long and by whom.
- Related services if needed (for example, speech-language therapy).
- Accommodations and modifications.
- How progress will be measured and reported to you.

You are an equal member of the IEP team. The IEP is reviewed at least annually, and your child is reevaluated at least every three years.

## The 504 plan

A **504 plan**, under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act, provides accommodations for students with disabilities that substantially limit a major life activity, such as reading or learning. A 504 plan typically provides accommodations such as extra time, audiobooks or assistive technology, but not specialized instruction.

| | IEP | 504 plan |
|---|---|---|
| Law | IDEA | Section 504 |
| Provides | Specialized instruction plus accommodations | Accommodations |
| Eligibility | One of IDEA's disability categories, needing specialized instruction | A disability that substantially limits a major life activity |
| Written goals | Yes, measurable annual goals | Not required |

Some children with dyslexia have a 504 plan; others need an IEP to receive specialized instruction.

## Outside school: tutoring and private intervention

Many families supplement school support with private help. Options include:

- **Reading specialists or certified tutors** trained in structured literacy approaches.
- **Learning centers** offering reading programs.
- **Speech-language pathologists** for children with underlying language difficulties.
- **Online tutoring** with qualified providers.

### Choosing a tutor

Ask:

- What training do you have in teaching reading? Are you trained in a structured literacy approach?
- How do you assess a child's needs before starting?
- What does a typical session include?
- How do you measure and report progress?
- How often do you recommend sessions? (Frequent sessions, such as two to four times a week, are generally more effective than once a week.)
- Can you coordinate with my child's school?

Be cautious of programs promising quick cures or relying on approaches without good evidence, such as vision training or colored lenses as a treatment for dyslexia.

## Questions to ask about any intervention

- What specific skills does it target?
- Is it explicit, systematic and cumulative?
- How often and for how long will my child receive it?
- What is the group size?
- How will progress be measured, and how often will I hear about it?
- What happens if my child isn't making progress?

## Supporting your child at home alongside help

- Keep reading aloud for enjoyment, vocabulary and knowledge.
- Practice the skills the interventionist recommends in short sessions.
- Use audiobooks for access to age-appropriate content.
- Celebrate effort and progress, and talk about strengths beyond reading.

## Advocating effectively

- **Keep records:** emails, evaluation reports, IEP or 504 documents, progress reports and work samples.
- **Ask for data:** progress monitoring results show whether support is working.
- **Know your rights:** read the procedural safeguards notice schools must provide.
- **Bring support:** you may bring a friend, relative or advocate to meetings.
- **Stay collaborative but persistent:** most problems are solved through respectful, persistent communication.

Parent training and information centers, funded in every US state, offer free help to families navigating special education.

## Comparing common types of support

| Type of support | Who provides it | Typical intensity | Best suited for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classroom differentiation | Classroom teacher | Daily, within regular lessons | Mild needs and early concerns |
| Small-group intervention | Reading specialist or trained teacher | Several times a week, 20 to 45 minutes | Children below benchmark who need targeted practice |
| Intensive intervention | Specialist | Daily, small group or one-to-one | Significant difficulties or slow response to earlier support |
| Special education services | Special education teacher, per an IEP | As specified in the plan | Children identified with a disability affecting reading |
| Private tutoring | Tutor or learning center | Usually one to three sessions a week | Extra practice, or support while school services are arranged |
| Speech-language therapy | Speech-language pathologist | As recommended after assessment | Underlying language or phonological difficulties |

## Tracking whether help is working

Any intervention should show measurable progress over time. Ask the school or tutor:

- What skills are being targeted, and how will progress be measured?
- How often will progress be checked?
- What rate of progress is expected?
- What will happen if my child is not making enough progress after a set period?

Keep a simple file with assessment results, reports and notes from meetings. Plotting progress-monitoring scores on a simple chart can help you see whether the gap is closing. If progress stalls, ask for the intervention to be adjusted or intensified rather than simply continued.

## Looking after your child's confidence

Children receiving extra help may feel embarrassed or worry that they are "not smart". Explain that everyone learns differently and that extra help is like coaching in a sport, a way to build skills faster. Celebrate effort and specific progress, protect time for activities your child excels at, and keep reading aloud together for pleasure so that books remain associated with enjoyment, not only with hard work. ## Frequently asked questions

### How long should I wait to see if intervention works?

Ask the school how often progress is monitored and what amount of progress is expected. If progress is insufficient after a reasonable period, often several weeks to a few months of consistent intervention, discuss next steps, including evaluation.

### Do private evaluations replace school evaluations?

No, but schools must consider them. They can provide valuable information.

### Can a child with an IEP still be in a regular classroom?

Yes. Most students with learning disabilities spend most of the day in general education classrooms, receiving specialized instruction for part of the day.

## In summary

Reading support in school ranges from classroom instruction and small-group intervention through MTSS to special education under IDEA, with IEPs providing specialized instruction and 504 plans providing accommodations. You can request an evaluation in writing at any time. Outside school, qualified tutors trained in structured literacy can add support. Keep records, ask for data, know your rights and keep reading aloud at home.
