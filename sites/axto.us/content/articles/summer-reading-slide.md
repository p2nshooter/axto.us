Every year as school ends, parents hear warnings about the "summer slide": the idea that children lose reading skills over the long break. Teachers often send home reading lists and logs, and families wonder how much to push reading during vacation.

The research on summer learning loss is more complicated than the headlines suggest, but one finding is consistent: children who keep reading over the summer, especially books they choose and enjoy, do better. This guide explains what is known, and offers practical ways to make summer reading something children look forward to.

## What the research says about the summer slide

Older studies, some dating back decades, reported that children's reading scores tended to stagnate or decline over the summer, with larger declines for children from lower-income families. That pattern led to concern that summer gaps could add up over years.

More recent research using newer test data has produced mixed results. Some studies find modest declines in reading over the summer; others find little loss, and researchers debate how much the measurements themselves affect the findings. What researchers broadly agree on is that:

- Summer is a time when differences in children's opportunities, such as access to books, libraries, camps and travel, can show up.
- Children who read regularly over the summer tend to maintain or improve their reading.
- Access to books matters: studies that gave children free books they chose for the summer have found benefits, particularly for children with fewer books at home.

So the practical message is clear even if the size of the slide is debated: keep children reading, and make books easy to get.

## The key ingredients: access, choice and enjoyment

### Access

Children cannot read books they do not have. Library cards, summer book giveaways, book swaps and a few well-chosen books at home make a big difference.

### Choice

Children read more when they choose what to read. Summer is a perfect time for comics, graphic novels, joke books, nonfiction about hobbies and series books, alongside any required reading.

### Enjoyment

Summer reading should feel like a pleasure, not more school. Pressure, rigid logs and boring assignments can turn children off.

## Practical ideas for summer reading

### Join the library's summer reading program

Most public libraries run free summer reading programs with reading challenges, small prizes, events, author visits and activities. They make reading social and fun, and they keep families visiting the library.

### Set up a family reading challenge

Create a challenge that suits your family: read a book in a hammock, a book by a new author, a book set in another country, a graphic novel, a book about an animal, a book recommended by a grandparent. Use a simple chart with stickers or stamps.

### Read aloud on vacation

Summer often brings more relaxed evenings. Start a longer family read-aloud and read a chapter every night. See [why you should keep reading aloud to older kids](/guides/reading-aloud-to-older-kids/).

### Use audiobooks on road trips

Long car journeys are perfect for family audiobooks. Choose something everyone will enjoy, and talk about it at stops. See [audiobooks for kids](/guides/audiobooks-for-kids/).

### Read about summer experiences

Before a trip to the beach, the mountains or a museum, find books about the place. Afterward, make a scrapbook with captions or write a travel journal.

### Create a reading nook outdoors

A blanket under a tree, a tent in the backyard or a porch chair with a basket of books makes reading feel like a summer treat.

### Make reading part of the daily rhythm

Even on vacation, a regular reading time, such as the quiet hour after lunch or the hour before bed, keeps the habit going.

### Mix in writing

Postcards to friends, a summer journal, a comic strip about the summer or a letter to a favorite author all reinforce literacy in playful ways.

## A summer reading bingo card

Many families enjoy a bingo-style challenge. Here is an example you can adapt:

| | | | |
|---|---|---|---|
| A book outdoors | A graphic novel | A book about an animal | A book from the library |
| A funny book | A book recommended by a friend | A book set in another country | A book of poems |
| A book read aloud by a grandparent | A nonfiction book about a hobby | A book with a map in it | A book in the car |
| A book by a new author | Reread a favorite | A book about summer | Your choice! |

Children color a square for each book. A completed row earns a family treat, like choosing the next library book or an evening picnic. Adjust the squares for younger or older readers.

## Building a summer book stack

Before school ends, spend an afternoon building a summer stack together:

1. Visit the library and let each child choose several books, including at least one "just for fun."
2. Ask the teacher or librarian for a few recommendations at your child's level.
3. Add one family read-aloud and one audiobook for trips.
4. Keep the stack in a special basket and refresh it every couple of weeks.

Having books ready on the first day of vacation makes it far more likely that reading becomes part of the summer routine.

## Required summer reading

If your child's school assigns summer reading:

- Start early and spread it out, rather than rushing in the last week of August.
- Read assigned books aloud together if they are challenging.
- Balance required reading with free-choice books.
- Talk about the books informally rather than quizzing.

## How much reading is enough?

Some studies of summer reading suggest that reading four or five books over the summer is associated with maintaining reading skills, though this is a rough guide, not a target to enforce. Twenty minutes a day of reading or being read to is a reasonable aim for most families. What matters most is regular reading that children enjoy.

## Summer reading for different ages

| Age | Summer reading ideas |
|---|---|
| Toddlers and preschoolers | Library story times, picture books outdoors, rhymes and songs on walks, bath books |
| Beginning readers (5–7) | Decodable books and early readers for practice, plus daily read-alouds of exciting stories |
| Developing readers (7–10) | Series and graphic novels, library reading challenges, audiobooks on trips |
| Older children (10+) | Free choice novels, nonfiction on passions, book clubs with friends, reading for summer projects |

## Supporting children who struggle

For children who find reading hard, summer can be an opportunity to practice without the pressure of school:

- Keep practice short and positive, focusing on skills the teacher recommends.
- Use decodable books and repeated reading for fluency. See [how to build reading fluency at home](/guides/reading-fluency-at-home/).
- Keep read-alouds and audiobooks going so their love of stories grows.
- Ask the school about summer programs or tutoring if your child needs more support.

## Myths about summer reading

**"Workbooks are the best way to prevent the summer slide."** Workbooks can help with specific skills, but reading real books that children enjoy is more motivating and builds broader skills.

**"Only 'challenging' books count."** Easy books build fluency and confidence. Graphic novels and series count too.

**"Summer should be a total break from reading."** A break from school is healthy, but reading for pleasure is part of a relaxing summer, not opposed to it.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I use rewards for summer reading?

Small celebrations, like library program prizes or a special outing, can motivate. Keep the focus on enjoyment rather than counting pages.

### What if my child refuses to read in summer?

Find books on their passions, try graphic novels or audiobooks, read aloud more, and model reading yourself. Avoid battles.

### Are summer reading logs useful?

Some children enjoy tracking their reading. For others, logs turn reading into a chore. Use them only if they motivate your child.

## In summary

Whether or not summer causes a large decline in reading, children who keep reading over the break do better. Make books accessible, let children choose, join the library's summer program, read aloud, use audiobooks on trips and keep reading enjoyable. A summer full of stories is the best protection against any slide.
