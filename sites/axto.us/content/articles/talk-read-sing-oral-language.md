Reading is often described as turning letters into sounds. That is part of it, but only half. A child who can sound out every word on a page still needs to understand what those words mean, how sentences fit together and what the story is about. That understanding comes from **oral language**: the words, sentences and knowledge a child has built through listening and talking long before learning to read.

This guide explains how spoken language and reading are connected, why everyday conversation matters so much, and simple ways to build a rich language environment at home from birth through the preschool years.

## Reading equals decoding times comprehension

A widely used model in reading research, the **Simple View of Reading**, proposed by Philip Gough and William Tunmer in 1986, describes reading comprehension as the product of two abilities:

- **Decoding:** recognizing written words, mainly by connecting letters to sounds.
- **Language comprehension:** understanding spoken language, including vocabulary, grammar and background knowledge.

The model is a multiplication, not an addition. If either ability is zero, comprehension is zero. A child who decodes perfectly but has a small vocabulary will struggle to understand texts; a child with a rich vocabulary who cannot decode will not access the text at all.

In the early years, before formal reading instruction, parents and caregivers have an enormous influence on the language comprehension side of that equation.

## The ingredients of oral language

Oral language is not one skill but several that grow together:

- **Vocabulary:** the number and depth of words a child knows.
- **Grammar and syntax:** how words combine into sentences, including more complex structures like "because" and "if."
- **Narrative skills:** the ability to tell and follow a sequence of events.
- **Background knowledge:** understanding of the world, which helps children make sense of new words and ideas.
- **Phonological awareness:** sensitivity to the sounds of language, such as rhymes and syllables, which links directly to learning phonics.

## Why conversation matters most

For years, discussions of early language focused on the number of words children hear. More recent research has highlighted something more specific: **conversational turns**, the back-and-forth exchanges between a child and an adult.

Studies using recording devices in homes have found that the number of conversational turns children experience is associated with language skills and with activity in brain regions involved in language processing. Hearing words matters, but taking part in conversations seems to matter even more.

A conversational turn can be as simple as:

- Baby coos, parent responds with words, baby coos again.
- Toddler points and says "Doggy!", parent says "Yes, a fluffy dog. He's wagging his tail!", toddler says "Wag!"
- Preschooler asks "Why is the moon up in the day?", parent and child talk about it.

You do not need special materials. You need attention and responsiveness.

## Talk: everyday ways to build language

### Narrate your day

Describe what you are doing and what your child is seeing: "I'm cutting the apple into slices. Crunch! Now we put them on your plate." This "parallel talk" gives children words for their experiences.

### Follow your child's lead

Notice what your child is interested in and talk about that. Children learn words best when the word connects to something they are already paying attention to.

### Expand and extend

When your child says something, repeat it back with a little more: "Car!" becomes "A blue car, driving fast." Later, extend with new ideas: "Where do you think it's going?"

### Ask open questions

Questions that begin with what, why, how and what if invite longer answers than yes/no questions.

### Use rich words

Don't simplify everything. Children enjoy interesting words, especially when you explain them in context: "That's an enormous pumpkin. Enormous means really, really big."

### Wait

Give children time to respond. Adults often jump in too quickly.

## Read: books bring new words

Books contain vocabulary and sentence patterns that are rare in everyday speech. Even simple picture books use words like "gigantic," "meadow" or "whispered" that may not come up during the day. Reading regularly and talking about the pictures and story multiplies a child's exposure to new language.

Shared reading also builds background knowledge. A child who has read about the ocean, farms, space or other cultures brings more understanding to future texts. ## Sing: songs and rhymes highlight sounds

Songs, nursery rhymes and finger plays slow language down and emphasize its sounds. Rhymes draw attention to word endings; clapping songs break words into syllables; alliterative phrases highlight beginning sounds. This builds phonological awareness, one of the strongest predictors of later reading success. ## Play: pretend play stretches language

Pretend play, such as playing house, shop or doctor, encourages children to use language to plan, negotiate and tell stories. Join in sometimes, taking a role and modeling new words: "I'd like to buy three oranges, please. How much do they cost?"

## Language in more than one language

Children can learn two or more languages from birth. Speaking to your child in the language you know best gives them the richest model of vocabulary and grammar. Skills built in one language, such as understanding stories and knowing many concepts, transfer to another. There is no need to switch to a language you speak less fluently.

## Screens and language

Background television reduces the amount of talk between parents and children, and young children learn language far less effectively from screens than from live interaction. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends avoiding screen media other than video chatting for children under 18 to 24 months, and limiting screen use for children aged two to five to about one hour a day of high-quality programming, ideally watched together and talked about.

## Signs of strong language development

Every child develops at their own pace, but encouraging signs include:

- Babbling with varied sounds by around 9 to 12 months.
- Using first words around 12 months and combining two words around 24 months.
- Asking many questions and telling simple stories by age three to four.
- Enjoying rhymes and noticing when words sound alike by age four to five.

If you have concerns about your child's speech or understanding, talk to your pediatrician. A hearing test and a speech-language evaluation can identify needs early, when support is most effective.

## A day of language, without extra time

Building oral language does not require a special lesson. Here is how ordinary routines can become rich language moments:

- **Breakfast:** "Do you want the crunchy cereal or the soft oatmeal? Which one is warmer?"
- **Getting dressed:** "First socks, then shoes. Your shoes have laces; mine have buckles."
- **In the car:** Play "I spy" or talk about where you are going and what you might see.
- **At the store:** Let your child find items on the list and describe them: "Find the biggest orange."
- **Cooking dinner:** Narrate steps, use words like stir, chop, sizzle and simmer.
- **Bath time:** Talk about floating and sinking, warm and cold, bubbles and splashes.
- **Bedtime:** Read a story, then talk about the best part of the day.

Each of these takes no more time than the routine itself, but together they add dozens of conversational turns and new words every day.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does talking to my baby really matter if they can't answer?

Yes. Babies learn by listening and by experiencing the rhythm of conversation long before they can talk back. Responding to their coos and babbles is the beginning of conversation.

### Is educational TV a good substitute?

Not for young children. Live interaction with a responsive adult is far more effective for language learning.

### My child is quiet. Should I worry?

Many children are quiet in some settings and talkative in others. What matters is their understanding and steady progress. If you're unsure, ask your pediatrician.

## In summary

Before children can read words, they need words to read. Talk with your child throughout the day, read books together and talk about them, and sing songs and rhymes. Every conversation builds the vocabulary, knowledge and sound awareness that will turn decoding into real reading later on.
