"Does listening to an audiobook count as reading?" is one of the most common questions parents ask. Some worry audiobooks are a shortcut that replaces "real" reading. Others have seen a reluctant child devour story after story through headphones and wonder whether that matters.

The honest answer is that listening and reading are different activities that share a great deal. Audiobooks offer real benefits, especially for vocabulary, comprehension and motivation, but they do not build decoding skills. This guide explains what research suggests, how audiobooks can help different children and how to use them well.

## Listening and reading: what's the same, what's different

When children read, they do two things: decode the words on the page and understand the language. When they listen, decoding is taken care of, and all their attention can go to understanding.

Research comparing listening and reading comprehension suggests that, for many people, understanding of a story is broadly similar whether they read it or hear it, especially for narrative texts. The language comprehension processes are shared. This fits the Simple View of Reading, which describes reading comprehension as decoding multiplied by language comprehension.

The differences:

- **Decoding practice:** only reading builds the ability to recognize written words.
- **Pacing:** a reader can slow down, reread or skim; a listener follows the narrator's pace, though they can pause and rewind.
- **Complex texts:** some studies suggest reading may support deeper comprehension of difficult expository texts, partly because readers can easily go back.
- **Spelling and written language:** reading exposes children to how words are spelled and how written language looks.

So audiobooks "count" for building language, vocabulary, knowledge and a love of stories. They don't replace practice in decoding print.

## The benefits of audiobooks

### Vocabulary and background knowledge

Audiobooks expose children to rich vocabulary and complex sentences, often from books above their independent reading level. Like read-alouds, they build the language comprehension side of reading.

### Access for struggling readers

For children with dyslexia or other reading difficulties, audiobooks provide access to age-appropriate stories and information that their decoding skills cannot yet reach. This keeps their vocabulary and knowledge growing and helps them participate in class discussions about books. Many schools use audiobooks as an accommodation for this reason.

### Modeling expressive reading

Skilled narrators demonstrate pacing, phrasing and expression, especially in performances with different voices for characters.

### Motivation

Audiobooks can rekindle interest in stories for reluctant readers and introduce children to series they then want to read in print.

### Practical convenience

Audiobooks fit into moments when reading isn't possible: car journeys, chores, quiet time, falling asleep.

## Read-along: the best of both

Listening to an audiobook while following the printed text, sometimes called read-along or immersion reading, combines the benefits. Children hear fluent, expressive reading while seeing the words, which can support word recognition and fluency. Some studies suggest this approach can help developing and struggling readers, particularly when combined with regular instruction.

Many libraries offer book-and-audio kits, and some e-books highlight words as they are read.

## Limits and cautions

- **Not a substitute for decoding practice.** Children learning to read still need to practice reading print with feedback.
- **Attention can drift.** Listening while doing something else can mean less is understood. Check in with conversation.
- **Content suitability.** Preview or check reviews, as with any book.
- **Screen time.** Audiobook apps on tablets and phones can lead to other screen use. Consider a dedicated player or a phone in airplane mode.

## Audiobooks for different children

| Child | How audiobooks can help |
|---|---|
| Preschoolers | Short stories, songs and rhymes at quiet time; never instead of shared reading |
| Beginning readers | Read-along books paired with print; stories above their decoding level |
| Struggling readers or dyslexia | Access to grade-level books and information; read-along for fluency support |
| Strong readers | Exposure to classics and complex books; more books than time allows |
| Children learning English | Models of pronunciation and natural rhythm; read-along to connect spoken and written English |
| Families on the go | Shared listening on car trips, followed by conversation |

## Getting audiobooks free or cheaply

- **Public libraries** lend audiobooks free through apps on phones and tablets, as well as CDs and audio kits.
- **Public-domain classics** are available free from volunteer recording projects.
- **School resources:** some schools provide access to audiobook platforms, especially for students with reading difficulties.

## Making audiobooks active, not passive

- **Listen together sometimes** and talk about the story.
- **Pause for predictions:** "What do you think happens next?"
- **Follow up:** draw a scene, act it out or find the print book at the library.
- **Discuss the narrator:** "How did the narrator make that character sound?"
- **Pair with print** when possible for read-along practice.

## A family audiobook routine

Here is one way to build audiobooks into family life without displacing other reading:

- **Car journeys:** a family audiobook for trips longer than twenty minutes, chosen by a different family member each time.
- **Quiet time:** younger children listen to a picture book with its audio while turning the pages.
- **Bedtime extension:** older children may listen to an audiobook for fifteen minutes after the shared read-aloud, if it helps them settle.
- **Weekend projects:** listen while building, drawing or cooking together, then talk about the story.

## Choosing a good audiobook

Not all audiobooks are equally engaging. When choosing, consider:

- **The narrator.** Listen to a sample. A warm, clear, expressive narrator can make a story; a flat one can ruin it. Full-cast productions with sound effects suit some children, while others prefer a single storyteller.
- **Length.** Young listeners do well with short books or collections of short stories. Long novels suit older children and long journeys.
- **Unabridged or abridged.** Unabridged versions keep the author's full language, which is better for vocabulary. Abridged versions can work for very long classics with younger listeners.
- **Pairing with print.** If your child is building fluency, choose titles available as both print and audio so they can follow along.
- **Interest first.** As with print, the best audiobook is one your child is excited to hear.

## Audiobooks in the classroom

Many teachers use audiobooks in class: to give every student access to a shared novel regardless of reading level, to support students with reading difficulties or those learning English, and to model fluent reading during independent reading time. If your child has a reading difficulty, ask whether audiobooks or text-to-speech are available as an accommodation for content-heavy subjects, so that decoding challenges do not prevent learning in science and history.

## Myths about audiobooks

**"Audiobooks are cheating."** They build language comprehension, vocabulary and knowledge, which are essential parts of reading. They just don't build decoding.

**"Kids who listen won't want to read."** Many children move from audiobooks to print books, especially in series they love.

**"Only struggling readers need them."** All children can benefit from the rich language and stories audiobooks offer.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should audiobooks count toward school reading logs?

That's a decision for the teacher. Many teachers count them as reading for enjoyment and comprehension, while expecting separate decoding practice.

### Can audiobooks help with dyslexia?

They provide access to content and support vocabulary and comprehension. They should be used alongside structured literacy instruction, not instead of it.

### At what age can children start audiobooks?

Short audio stories can be enjoyed from the preschool years. Longer audiobooks suit children who can follow extended stories, often from around five or six.

## In summary

Audiobooks "count" for building vocabulary, background knowledge, comprehension and a love of stories, and they are invaluable for struggling readers and busy families. They do not build decoding, so children learning to read still need practice with print. Use them actively, pair them with print when you can, and enjoy the stories together.
