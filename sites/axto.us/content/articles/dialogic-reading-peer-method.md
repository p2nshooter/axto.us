When most adults read to a young child, they do the talking and the child listens. Dialogic reading flips that balance. The adult becomes a questioner and an active listener, and the child gradually becomes the storyteller. It is one of the most studied ways of sharing books with preschoolers, and it requires nothing but a picture book and a few simple techniques.

This guide explains where dialogic reading comes from, how the PEER sequence and CROWD prompts work, and how to use them at home or in a classroom without turning story time into a quiz.

## Where dialogic reading comes from

Dialogic reading was developed in the late 1980s by psychologist Grover Whitehurst and colleagues at Stony Brook University. Their studies found that when parents of two- and three-year-olds were taught to ask open-ended questions and expand on their children's answers during picture-book reading, the children's expressive vocabulary grew more than that of children whose parents read in the usual way. Later studies extended the approach to preschool classrooms and to children from a range of backgrounds.

Reviews of the research generally find positive effects on oral language, especially vocabulary, for children around ages two to five. The effects tend to be larger for younger children and when the technique is used consistently. Dialogic reading is not a cure-all, but it is a well-supported, low-cost way to make shared reading more powerful.

## The core idea

In ordinary reading, the book does the work. In dialogic reading, the **conversation** does the work. The goal is for the child to talk as much as possible about the story and pictures, while the adult prompts, responds and gently adds new words and ideas.

A useful rule of thumb: by the end of a session, the child should have said a lot, not just heard a lot.

## The PEER sequence

PEER is a short loop you repeat throughout the book:

1. **Prompt** the child to say something about the book.
2. **Evaluate** the child's response.
3. **Expand** the response by rephrasing it and adding information.
4. **Repeat** the prompt so the child can use the expanded language.

### An example

Imagine a picture of a girl holding a red umbrella in the rain.

- **Prompt:** "What's this?" (pointing at the umbrella)
- **Child:** "Umbrella."
- **Evaluate:** "That's right!"
- **Expand:** "It's a red umbrella. She's holding it because it's raining."
- **Repeat:** "Can you say that? What is she holding?"
- **Child:** "A red umbrella. It's raining."

The child has gone from one word to a full idea, using new vocabulary in their own voice.

### Evaluating kindly

Evaluating does not mean grading. If the child's answer is not quite right, model the correct idea warmly: "That looks a bit like a hat, doesn't it? It's actually an umbrella. People hold umbrellas when it rains." Avoid saying "No, that's wrong," which can make children reluctant to answer.

## The CROWD prompts

CROWD describes five kinds of prompts to use in the first step of PEER. Varying them keeps the conversation interesting and stretches different skills.

| Prompt | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **C**ompletion | Child fills in a blank, often a rhyme or repeated phrase | "Brown bear, brown bear, what do you ___?" |
| **R**ecall | Child remembers what happened | "What did the caterpillar eat on Monday?" |
| **O**pen-ended | Child describes a picture in their own words | "Tell me what's happening on this page." |
| **W**h- questions | What, where, when, why, who | "Why do you think the dog is hiding?" |
| **D**istancing | Child connects the book to their own life | "Have you ever been to a farm like this?" |

### Matching prompts to age

- **Two-year-olds:** completion prompts and simple "what" questions about pictures work best.
- **Three-year-olds:** add recall, "where" and more open-ended prompts.
- **Four- and five-year-olds:** "why" questions, predictions and distancing prompts invite longer, more complex answers.

## Step by step: a dialogic reading session

1. **Choose a book with rich pictures.** Books where a lot happens in each illustration give plenty to talk about. Very text-heavy books are harder to use.
2. **Read it normally first.** Children enjoy hearing the whole story before being asked about it. Use dialogic techniques on second and later readings.
3. **Prompt on most pages, not every line.** One or two prompts per page keeps the story flowing.
4. **Follow the child's interest.** If your child wants to talk about the cat in the corner instead of the main character, go with it.
5. **Praise effort, expand language.** Every response is an opportunity to add a word or idea.
6. **Keep it short and fun.** Ten minutes is plenty for most preschoolers. Stop before interest fades.

## Common mistakes

- **Turning it into a test.** Rapid-fire questions with right and wrong answers feel like an exam. Keep a warm, curious tone.
- **Asking only yes/no questions.** "Is that a dog?" invites one word. "What's the dog doing?" invites a sentence.
- **Not waiting long enough.** Young children need several seconds to think and form words. Count silently to five.
- **Doing all the expanding at once.** Add one new word or idea at a time.
- **Using it on every reading.** Sometimes children just want to hear the story. Mix dialogic sessions with simple read-alouds.

## Dialogic reading for children learning two languages

Dialogic reading works in any language. For children growing up bilingual, parents can use it in whichever language they speak most richly. Strong vocabulary and story skills in the home language support later learning in the school language.

## Using it in a classroom or small group

Teachers can use dialogic reading with small groups of three to five children so each child gets turns to talk. Some practical tips:

- Use the same book for several days so children become familiar with it.
- Write a few CROWD prompts on sticky notes inside the cover.
- Rotate who answers, and let children prompt each other once they understand the pattern.
- Send the book home with a short note so families can try the same prompts.

## What dialogic reading is not

Dialogic reading builds oral language: vocabulary, sentence complexity and the ability to talk about stories. It is not a method for teaching children to decode written words. Learning to read print requires phonemic awareness and phonics, which we explain in our guides to [phonological awareness](/guides/nursery-rhymes-phonological-awareness/) and [what phonics is](/guides/what-is-phonics/). The two work together: strong oral language helps children understand the words they will later learn to decode.

## A worked example with a familiar book

Imagine reading a picture book about a bear looking for his lost hat for the third time with a four-year-old. A dialogic exchange on one page might go like this:

- **Adult (open-ended prompt):** "Tell me what's happening here."
- **Child:** "The bear is asking the rabbit."
- **Adult (evaluate and expand):** "Yes! The bear is asking the rabbit if he has seen his hat. The rabbit looks nervous, doesn't he?"
- **Adult (repeat):** "What is the bear asking?"
- **Child:** "If he saw his hat. The rabbit is nervous."
- **Adult (wh- prompt):** "Why do you think the rabbit looks nervous?"
- **Child:** "Because... he has the hat!"
- **Adult (distancing prompt):** "Have you ever lost something special? How did you find it?"

In about a minute, the child has described a scene, used new words like "nervous," made an inference and connected the story to their own life. That is the power of the method.

## Frequently asked questions

### At what age can I start dialogic reading?

Simple versions work from around age two, when children have enough words to answer. Before that, naming pictures and responding to babbles lays the groundwork.

### What if my child won't answer?

Model the answer yourself and try again later. Some children need to hear the pattern many times before joining in. Completion prompts on a favorite book are often the easiest way in.

### Can older children benefit?

The principles of conversation, open questions and expanding answers help at any age, though the specific CROWD structure is designed for preschoolers.

## In summary

Dialogic reading turns story time into a conversation. Use the PEER loop to prompt, evaluate kindly, expand and repeat, and vary your prompts with CROWD. Choose picture-rich books, keep sessions short and playful and let your child do more and more of the talking. It is one of the simplest, best-supported ways to grow a young child's language through books.
