Reading is ultimately about understanding. A child who decodes every word perfectly but cannot tell you what a story was about has not really read it. Yet comprehension is harder to teach than decoding, because it draws on so many things at once: vocabulary, background knowledge, memory, attention and the ability to connect ideas.

This guide explains what reading comprehension depends on, which strategies research supports and how to weave them naturally into reading at home, without turning every book into a quiz.

## What comprehension depends on

Comprehension is not a single skill. It rests on several foundations:

- **Fluent word reading.** If decoding is slow and effortful, there is little attention left for meaning. See our guide to [reading fluency](/guides/reading-fluency-at-home/).
- **Vocabulary.** Children need to know the meanings of most words in a text, research suggests something like 95 percent or more, to understand it well.
- **Background knowledge.** Knowing about the topic makes texts much easier to understand. A child who knows a lot about baseball will understand a story about a baseball game better than a stronger reader who knows nothing about the sport.
- **Language skills.** Understanding sentence structures, pronouns and connectives like "although" and "because."
- **Active engagement.** Good readers think while they read: they predict, ask questions, notice when something doesn't make sense and fix it.

Scarborough's Reading Rope, a model widely used by educators, describes reading as two strands woven together: word recognition (phonological awareness, decoding, sight recognition) and language comprehension (background knowledge, vocabulary, language structures, verbal reasoning and literacy knowledge). Both strands must be strong for skilled reading.

## Why background knowledge matters so much

Studies have found that readers with more knowledge about a topic comprehend texts on that topic better, sometimes regardless of their general reading ability. This has a practical implication: one of the best ways to improve comprehension is to build children's knowledge of the world through rich reading, conversation, experiences and nonfiction.

That is also why reading aloud remains so important after children can read alone. Read-alouds expose children to ideas, vocabulary and topics beyond what they can read independently.

## Strategies that help

Research on comprehension instruction supports teaching children a small number of strategies explicitly, then practicing them with real texts. Here are the most useful, with ways to use them at home.

### 1. Activating prior knowledge

Before reading, talk about what your child already knows. "This book is about volcanoes. What do you know about volcanoes?" Connecting new information to existing knowledge helps it stick.

### 2. Predicting

Ask your child to predict what might happen based on the title, cover or what has happened so far. "What do you think the fox will do next?" Afterward, check: "Were you right? What surprised you?" Predicting keeps children actively engaged.

### 3. Asking questions

Good readers wonder as they read. Model your own questions: "I wonder why she didn't tell her brother." Encourage your child to ask questions too, about characters, events or facts. Questions that start with why and how lead to deeper thinking.

### 4. Visualizing

Encourage your child to make pictures in their mind. "What do you imagine the castle looks like?" With books that have few illustrations, drawing a scene can help.

### 5. Monitoring and fixing up

Skilled readers notice when they lose track and do something about it: reread, slow down, look up a word, ask for help. Teach your child to stop when something doesn't make sense: "Does that make sense? Let's reread that part."

### 6. Making inferences

Authors don't say everything directly. Help your child read between the lines: "The book doesn't say he's nervous, but how can we tell?" Inference questions build deeper understanding.

### 7. Summarizing and retelling

After reading, ask your child to retell the story in their own words: who, what, where, the problem, how it was solved. For nonfiction, ask for the main idea and a few important details. Retelling shows what your child understood and strengthens memory.

### 8. Understanding text structure

Stories have a structure: characters, setting, problem, events, resolution. Nonfiction has different structures: cause and effect, compare and contrast, sequence, problem and solution. Pointing these out helps children organize what they read.

## Building vocabulary

Vocabulary is so central to comprehension that it deserves its own attention:

- **Talk about interesting words** you meet in books: "Gigantic. That's even bigger than big!"
- **Explain words in child-friendly language** with examples.
- **Use new words in conversation** over the following days.
- **Read widely,** including nonfiction and books slightly above your child's reading level, read aloud.
- **Play with word parts:** prefixes like un- and re-, suffixes like -ful and -less.

## Comprehension conversations at home

Rather than asking a list of questions after a book, weave a few into natural conversation:

| Before reading | During reading | After reading |
|---|---|---|
| What do you think this is about? | What do you think will happen next? | What was your favorite part and why? |
| What do you already know about this? | Why do you think she did that? | What was the problem, and how was it solved? |
| Look at the pictures. What do you notice? | How would you feel if you were him? | Did anything surprise you? |
| | Does that make sense? | What did you learn that you didn't know before? |

Two or three questions per book are enough. Comprehension conversations should feel like talking about a book you both enjoyed.

## Comprehension and nonfiction

Nonfiction requires slightly different strategies: using headings, captions, diagrams and glossaries; distinguishing main ideas from details; and connecting facts. Many children love nonfiction, and it builds the background knowledge that supports all reading. See [why kids need nonfiction too](/guides/nonfiction-for-kids/).

## A worked example

Imagine reading a short story about a girl who loses her dog in a storm with an eight-year-old:

- **Before:** "The title is *Lost in the Storm*. What do you think might happen? Have you ever been caught in a storm?"
- **During:** "The author says the sky turned green. What do you picture? Why do you think the dog ran away?"
- **Monitoring:** "Wait, I'm confused. Who is Marcus? Let's look back. Oh, he's her neighbor."
- **Inference:** "She keeps checking the window. How is she feeling?"
- **After:** "Can you tell me the story in a few sentences? What did she do that helped find the dog? What would you have done?"

In a few minutes of natural conversation, the child has predicted, visualized, monitored, inferred and summarized.

## Myths about comprehension

**"Comprehension will come once decoding is fluent."** Fluent decoding is necessary but not sufficient. Vocabulary and knowledge must grow alongside it.

**"Teaching strategies is enough."** Strategy instruction helps, but children also need rich content. A child who knows the "main idea" strategy still struggles with a text on a topic they know nothing about.

**"Asking lots of questions improves comprehension."** Too many questions can make reading feel like a test. A few thoughtful questions in a genuine conversation work better.

## When to seek help

If your child reads fluently but consistently struggles to understand, or understands well when listening but not when reading, talk to the teacher. Comprehension difficulties can stem from vocabulary or language weaknesses, attention difficulties or a mismatch between reading level and text difficulty. An evaluation can identify the cause. See [signs your child may be struggling with reading](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/).

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I tell if my child understood a book?

Ask them to retell it in their own words. A good retelling includes the main characters, the problem and how it was solved, in a sensible order.

### Should my child read books that are too hard?

For independent reading, books should be mostly comfortable. Harder books are excellent for reading aloud, where you can support understanding.

### Do worksheets help comprehension?

Some can, but conversations about books your child cares about are usually more engaging and effective.

## In summary

Reading comprehension depends on fluent decoding, rich vocabulary, broad background knowledge and active thinking. Build knowledge through wide reading and conversation, teach a few strategies such as predicting, questioning, visualizing, monitoring and summarizing, and weave them into natural talk about books. The goal is a child who reads not just words, but meaning.
