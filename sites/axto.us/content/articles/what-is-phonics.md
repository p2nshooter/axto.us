If your child is starting school, you will soon hear the word **phonics**. You may see it on homework sheets, in reading books that look oddly repetitive and in conversations about how reading is taught. Phonics can sound technical, but the idea at its heart is simple, and understanding it will help you support your child with confidence.

This guide explains what phonics is, why most reading researchers consider it essential, how a systematic phonics program usually progresses and what you can do at home.

## The basic idea

English is written with an alphabet: letters and groups of letters represent the sounds of spoken words. **Phonics** is teaching children those connections, so that they can:

- **Decode:** look at a written word, turn the letters into sounds and blend the sounds into the word ("c-a-t" → "cat").
- **Encode:** hear a spoken word, break it into sounds and write the letters for each sound, which is spelling.

A few terms are useful:

- **Phoneme:** the smallest unit of sound in a word. "Ship" has three phonemes: /sh/ /i/ /p/.
- **Grapheme:** the letter or letters that represent a phoneme. In "ship," the graphemes are "sh," "i" and "p."
- **Blending:** combining sounds to read a word.
- **Segmenting:** breaking a word into its sounds to spell it.

## Why phonics matters

Children cannot memorize every word they will ever read as a picture; there are simply too many. Phonics gives them a tool to work out unfamiliar words independently. Once children decode a word several times, it becomes stored in memory and is recognized instantly, a process researchers call **orthographic mapping**. Phonics is the route to that automatic word recognition, which frees attention for understanding.

The National Reading Panel's 2000 report concluded that systematic phonics instruction significantly improves children's reading, particularly in kindergarten and first grade, and benefits children from all backgrounds, including those at risk of reading difficulties. Many later reviews have reached similar conclusions, and phonics is central to what is often called the science of reading. ## Systematic and explicit

Not all phonics teaching is the same. Research supports phonics that is:

- **Systematic:** skills are taught in a planned sequence, from simple to complex, rather than whenever they happen to come up.
- **Explicit:** the teacher directly shows the sound-letter relationship ("these two letters, s-h, make the sound /sh/") rather than expecting children to discover it on their own.
- **Cumulative:** each new skill builds on what has been learned, with lots of review.
- **Applied:** children practice new skills right away by reading and writing words and sentences that use them.

## A typical phonics sequence

Programs vary, but most follow a similar progression. This is an approximate picture of how skills build in kindergarten through second grade:

| Stage | What children learn | Example words |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Single letter sounds | Common sounds for individual letters, starting with high-utility ones | s, a, t, p, i, n |
| 2. CVC words | Blending consonant–vowel–consonant words | sat, pin, map, dog |
| 3. Digraphs | Two letters, one sound | ship, chin, thin, duck, sing |
| 4. Blends | Two or three consonants, each heard | stop, frog, jump, splash |
| 5. Long vowels with silent e | The "magic e" pattern | cake, bike, home, cute |
| 6. Vowel teams | Two vowels representing one sound | rain, boat, feet, play |
| 7. R-controlled vowels | Vowels changed by r | car, bird, fork, her |
| 8. Other patterns | Diphthongs, soft c and g, common endings | coin, cow, city, jumping |
| 9. Multisyllable words | Syllable types and dividing longer words | rabbit, sunset, picnic |

Alongside these, children learn a small number of **high-frequency words** with irregular spellings, such as "the," "said" and "was." Many programs now teach these as "heart words," focusing on the parts that are regular and memorizing only the tricky part. See our guide to [sight words and heart words](/guides/sight-words-and-heart-words/).

## What a phonics lesson looks like

A typical lesson in a structured program might include:

1. **Review** of previously learned sounds, often with flashcards.
2. **Introduction** of a new sound and its spelling, with a picture or action to help memory.
3. **Blending practice:** reading words that contain the new sound.
4. **Segmenting and spelling:** writing words from dictation.
5. **Connected text:** reading a short [decodable book](/guides/decodable-books-explained/) or sentences using the sounds learned so far.

Lessons are usually short, frequent and lively, often 20 to 30 minutes daily.

## How to support phonics at home

You do not need to be a teacher to help. Short, positive practice makes a big difference.

### Learn the sounds the school uses

Ask your child's teacher which program they use and how they say the sounds. Saying /m/ rather than "muh" keeps blending simple. Many programs share videos or sound charts for families.

### Practice blending with games

Say words in separate sounds and let your child blend them: "What's /p/ /i/ /g/?" Turn it around and let your child give you sounds to blend.

### Read decodable books together

When your child brings home a book matched to the sounds they have learned, let them do the decoding. Encourage them to sound out unfamiliar words rather than guess from pictures.

### Make words with letters

Use magnetic letters or letter tiles to build words, then change one letter to make a new word: cat, hat, hot, hop.

### Play spelling games

Say a word and ask your child to tap out the sounds, then write it. Celebrate logical attempts: "kat" for "cat" shows your child heard every sound.

### Keep reading aloud

While your child practices decoding simple texts, keep reading richer books aloud. Phonics builds decoding; read-alouds build vocabulary and comprehension.

## What to avoid

- **Encouraging guessing from pictures or the first letter.** Some older methods taught children to guess words from context or pictures. Researchers now generally advise against this, because it does not build the decoding skills children need for harder texts.
- **Long drill sessions.** Five to ten minutes of focused, fun practice is better than half an hour of frustration.
- **Comparing with siblings or friends.** Children progress at different rates.

## When phonics doesn't seem to click

Most children learn to decode with good instruction and practice, but some need more time and more explicit teaching. Talk to your child's teacher if your child:

- Still struggles to connect common letters to sounds by the middle of kindergarten.
- Has great difficulty blending simple words by the end of kindergarten.
- Seems to memorize words without being able to sound out new ones.
- Becomes very anxious or avoids reading.

Persistent difficulty with phonics can be a sign of dyslexia or other learning differences, and early intervention is highly effective. See our guide to [signs of reading difficulty](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/).

## Questions to ask your child's school

- Which phonics program do you use, and is it systematic and explicit?
- What sounds and words is my child working on now?
- How do you check whether my child is making progress?
- How can I support the program at home?
- What happens if my child needs extra help?

## Frequently asked questions

### Is phonics enough to teach reading?

Phonics is essential but not sufficient. Children also need phonemic awareness, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension, along with lots of reading and being read to.

### My child learned to read early without phonics. Is that a problem?

Some children work out letter-sound patterns on their own. It is still worth making sure they can decode unfamiliar words, which supports spelling and reading harder texts later.

### English spelling is so irregular. Does phonics really work?

English has many irregularities, but most words follow predictable patterns, and even irregular words are usually regular in most of their letters. Phonics gives children tools for the large majority of words.

## In summary

Phonics teaches children how letters represent sounds so they can decode and spell words. Systematic, explicit phonics, taught in a planned sequence with plenty of practice, is one of the best-supported elements of early reading instruction. Support it at home with short games, decodable books and plenty of read-alouds, and talk to the school early if your child is struggling.
