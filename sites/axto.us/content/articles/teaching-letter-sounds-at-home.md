Knowing letter sounds is one of the first building blocks of reading. A child who can look at the letter "m" and immediately think /m/ is ready to start blending sounds into words. The good news is that letter sounds can be learned through play in a few minutes a day, without workbooks, flashcard battles or tears.

This guide explains how to teach letter sounds at home in a way that matches how schools teach phonics, with the right order, the right pronunciation and games your child will actually enjoy.

## Letter names and letter sounds

Children often learn letter names first, partly thanks to the alphabet song. Letter names are useful, but for reading, **letter sounds** matter more. The letter "w" is called "double-u," but in words it makes the sound /w/. The letter "h" is called "aitch," but it says /h/.

Both are worth learning, and many children learn them side by side. When you are helping your child read, though, focus on sounds. If your child says the letter names while trying to read "cat," they will hear "see-ay-tee," which does not blend into anything.

## Say the sounds cleanly

One of the most helpful things you can do is say letter sounds correctly, without adding an extra "uh" sound at the end. This is sometimes called saying "pure sounds."

| Letter | Say this | Avoid this |
|---|---|---|
| m | /mmm/ (hum it) | "muh" |
| s | /sss/ (like a snake) | "suh" |
| f | /fff/ | "fuh" |
| t | /t/ (quick and quiet) | "tuh" |
| p | /p/ (a puff of air) | "puh" |
| b | /b/ (short) | "buh" |

Some sounds, like /m/, /s/ and /f/, can be stretched. Others, like /t/, /p/ and /b/, are quick "stop" sounds. Saying them crisply makes blending much easier: /m/ /a/ /t/ blends into "mat," while "muh-a-tuh" sounds like a different word.

Ask your child's school how they say the sounds. Many phonics programs share videos for families.

## What order to teach

You don't need to go from A to Z. Most phonics programs start with letters that:

- Are very common in simple words.
- Are easy to say and to distinguish.
- Can quickly be combined into real words.

A common starting set is **s, a, t, p, i, n**. With just those six, children can read and build words like sat, pat, tap, pin, tip, nap, sit and pan. That early success is very motivating.

After that, programs typically add letters like m, d, g, o, c, k, e, u, r, h, b, f, l, then the less common ones such as j, v, w, x, y, z and qu.

For short vowels, teach one clear sound first: /a/ as in apple, /e/ as in egg, /i/ as in insect, /o/ as in octopus, /u/ as in umbrella. Other vowel sounds come later.

## Games that build letter sounds

### Sound of the week

Choose one sound and look for it everywhere: at breakfast ("banana starts with /b/!"), on walks, in books. Keep a list on the fridge of things you find.

### Mystery bag

Put a few small objects in a bag. Your child pulls one out, names it and says its first sound. Later, sort objects into piles by first sound.

### Letter hunt

Hide magnetic letters or letter cards around a room. When your child finds one, they say its sound before collecting it.

### Sound actions

Many phonics programs pair each sound with an action: wiggling fingers like ants for /a/, a snake movement for /s/. Actions make sounds memorable for young children who learn through movement.

### Letter in the sand

Write letters in sand, shaving foam, salt on a tray or with a finger on your child's back while saying the sound. Tracing the shape while saying the sound links movement, sight and sound.

### Quick flash game

Once your child knows several sounds, flash letter cards for a few seconds and see how quickly they can say the sound. Keep it to a minute or two and include mostly sounds they know well, so they feel successful.

### Build a word

As soon as your child knows a few sounds, start building simple words with letter tiles: s-a-t. Say each sound, then blend them together. This is the moment letter sounds turn into reading.

## How long and how often

Short and frequent is the rule. Five to ten minutes a day, ideally playful and at a time when your child is not tired or hungry, works far better than long sessions. Stop while your child is still enjoying it.

Most children learn a new sound in a few days of playful exposure, but some need more repetition. Keep reviewing old sounds as you add new ones; quick, confident recall is the goal.

## Uppercase and lowercase

Most books use lowercase letters, so they are more important for reading. Many phonics programs introduce lowercase first, with uppercase taught alongside or soon after. Point out uppercase letters in your child's name and at the start of sentences.

## Linking sounds to writing

Encourage your child to write the letters as they learn the sounds. Even scribbled attempts help. Saying the sound while writing the letter strengthens memory.

Encourage spelling attempts: if your child writes "dg" for "dog," celebrate the sounds they heard and gently show the missing one. Inventive spelling that reflects sounds is a sign of growing phonemic awareness.

## When letters are confusing

Some letters are easily mixed up, especially b and d, and p and q. This is very common in young children and usually resolves with time. You can help by:

- Teaching one of the pair thoroughly before the other.
- Using a memory aid, such as the word "bed" where the b and d form the headboard and footboard of a bed.
- Practicing the formation of each letter while saying its sound.

Reversals alone are not a sign of dyslexia in young children.

## When to seek advice

Talk to your child's teacher if, by the middle to end of kindergarten, your child:

- Knows very few letter sounds despite regular practice.
- Has trouble remembering sounds from one day to the next.
- Struggles to hear the first sound in spoken words.

Some children need more explicit, systematic teaching and more practice. Early support works best. See our guide to [signs of reading difficulty](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/).

## A sample week of five-minute practice

Short, playful sessions work better than long drills. Here is one way to structure a week while introducing two new sounds, for example /m/ and /s/, alongside review.

| Day | Activity (about five minutes) |
|---|---|
| Monday | Introduce /m/: show the letter, say the sound, find things in the kitchen that start with /m/ |
| Tuesday | Review /m/, introduce /s/: snake hissing game, trace the letter in sand or flour |
| Wednesday | Sound hunt: hide cards for m, s and two known letters around the room; your child says each sound when found |
| Thursday | Writing: your child writes m and s while saying the sounds, then you dictate the sounds for them to write |
| Friday | Blending fun: with known letters, build simple words like "sat" or "mat" with magnetic letters and blend them together |

Keep a small box of letter cards your child has learned, and celebrate when it grows. If a sound is not sticking, keep it in daily review rather than moving on too fast.

## Using everyday moments

Letter sounds do not have to be practiced only at the table. Point out the first sound of street signs, cereal boxes and shop names. Play "I spy something that starts with /b/" on walks. Ask your child to find the first letter of their name on car number plates. These brief moments add up to a lot of practice without feeling like lessons.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I teach letter sounds before school?

There's no need to rush. Many children enjoy learning sounds in preschool, but plenty of talk, reading and rhyme games are just as important at that age.

### What about letters that make more than one sound?

Teach one main sound first, usually the short vowel sound or the most common consonant sound. Other sounds will come later in phonics instruction.

### Are alphabet apps useful?

Some can offer extra practice, but many teach letter names more than sounds or reward fast tapping over learning. Playful practice with you is more effective.

## In summary

Focus on letter sounds, not just names, and say them cleanly without an extra "uh." Start with a small set like s, a, t, p, i, n so your child can read real words quickly. Keep practice short, playful and frequent, link sounds to writing and talk to the school if progress is slow. A few minutes of games a day can turn letters into reading.
