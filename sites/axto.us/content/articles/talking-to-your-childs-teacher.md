If you are worried about your child's reading, the teacher is usually the best first person to talk to. Teachers see your child read every day, have assessment information you may not, and know what support the school can offer. Yet many parents hesitate: they don't want to seem pushy, they feel unsure about the terminology, or they worry about being told everything is fine when it doesn't feel fine.

This guide explains how to prepare for the conversation, what to ask, how to understand what you hear and how to build a partnership that helps your child.

## When to reach out

Contact the teacher if:

- You notice signs of reading difficulty at home. See [signs your child may be struggling with reading](/guides/signs-of-reading-difficulty/).
- Your child is anxious about reading or school.
- Homework involving reading takes much longer than expected or ends in tears.
- Report cards or screening results concern you.
- There is a family history of dyslexia and you notice early difficulties.

You don't need to wait for a scheduled conference. Most teachers welcome early contact.

## Preparing for the conversation

### Write down your observations

Note specific examples: "She guesses words from the first letter," "He can't sound out three-letter words," "Reading homework takes 45 minutes and ends in tears." Specific observations are more helpful than general worries.

### Bring work samples

Samples of your child's writing or notes on how reading at home goes can help the teacher see what you see.

### Note family history

If a parent or sibling had reading difficulties or dyslexia, mention it. Family history is a risk factor.

### List your questions

Write down questions in advance so you don't forget them.

## Questions to ask

### About your child's skills

- How is my child doing in phonemic awareness, phonics, fluency, vocabulary and comprehension?
- What screening or assessment results do you have? What do they mean?
- How does my child compare with grade-level expectations?
- What do you see as my child's strengths?

### About instruction and support

- What reading instruction does my child receive? Is phonics taught systematically and explicitly?
- Is my child receiving any extra support? How often, by whom, and in what group size?
- What specific skills does the support target?
- How is progress monitored, and how often?

### About next steps

- What can I do at home to support what you're doing in class?
- When should we meet again to review progress?
- If my child doesn't make progress, what is the next step?
- Would an evaluation for special education services be appropriate?

## Understanding what you hear

Teachers may use terms like these:

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Universal screening | Brief tests given to all students to identify those at risk |
| Benchmark | The expected score or level for a point in the year |
| Progress monitoring | Frequent short assessments to check whether support is working |
| Tier 2 / Tier 3 | Levels of extra support in a multi-tiered system (small group, then more intensive) |
| MTSS / RTI | Multi-Tiered System of Supports / Response to Intervention: frameworks for providing increasing levels of help |
| Intervention | Targeted instruction to address specific skill gaps |
| IEP | Individualized Education Program for students who qualify for special education |
| 504 plan | A plan providing accommodations for students with disabilities who don't need specialized instruction |

Don't hesitate to ask the teacher to explain any term.

## Building a partnership

### Assume good intentions

Most teachers care deeply about their students' progress. Approach the conversation as a partnership: "I've noticed some things at home and I'd love to understand what you're seeing."

### Share your knowledge

You know your child best: their history, interests, frustrations and what motivates them. That information helps the teacher.

### Agree on a plan

Leave the meeting with clear next steps: what the school will do, what you will do at home and when you'll check in again.

### Follow up in writing

Send a brief email summarizing what you discussed and agreed. This creates a record and ensures shared understanding.

### Keep communication going

Check in regularly, especially if your child is receiving extra support.

## If you're told not to worry

Sometimes a teacher may suggest waiting. That may be reasonable if your child is making steady progress. But if you're still concerned:

- Ask what specific evidence suggests your child is on track.
- Ask what the plan is if progress doesn't come.
- Set a specific date to review progress.
- Know that you have the right to request an evaluation for special education services in writing. See [reading help options](/guides/reading-help-options/).

## Requesting an evaluation

Under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), parents can request an evaluation if they suspect their child has a disability affecting learning. Put the request in writing, date it and keep a copy. The school must respond, and if it agrees to evaluate, it needs your written consent. Federal law sets a timeline for completing the evaluation after consent (generally 60 days, unless your state sets its own timeline). If the school declines, it must explain why in writing, and you have options to dispute the decision.

## Supporting your child through the process

- Keep reading aloud and reading for pleasure at home.
- Practice the skills the teacher recommends in short sessions.
- Talk positively about school support: "Ms. Lopez is helping you learn the tricky sounds."
- Protect your child's confidence by emphasizing effort, progress and strengths.

## A sample email to the teacher

> Dear Ms. Rivera,
>
> I'd like to talk about Maya's reading. At home I've noticed that she often guesses words from the first letter and finds it hard to sound out short words like "sat" or "pin." Reading homework has been taking about 40 minutes and she gets very upset. Her older brother had reading difficulties too.
>
> Could we find a time to meet? I'd love to hear what you're seeing in class, any screening results and how I can help at home.
>
> Thank you,
> Sam Patel

## Making the most of parent-teacher conferences

Scheduled conferences are often short, sometimes ten or fifteen minutes, so preparation pays off.

### Before the conference

- Write down your two or three most important questions.
- Bring examples: a page your child struggled with, a writing sample, notes on how long homework takes.
- Ask your child how they feel about reading and school, and note anything they want you to share.

### During the conference

- Start with your top concern so it is not squeezed out at the end.
- Ask to see your child's work and any assessment results, and ask what they mean.
- Ask what the teacher sees in class: participation, confidence, how your child reads aloud.
- Agree on one or two concrete next steps, at school and at home.

### After the conference

- Send a short thank-you email summarizing what you agreed.
- Put a date on your calendar to check in again, perhaps in four to six weeks.
- Tell your child, in encouraging terms, what you and the teacher are going to do together.

## Understanding common assessment terms

Teachers may use terms that are unfamiliar. A few common ones:

| Term | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| Benchmark or screener | A brief test given to all students to identify who may need extra support |
| Progress monitoring | Frequent short checks to see whether an intervention is working |
| Percentile | How a score compares with other children of the same age or grade; the 30th percentile means scoring higher than 30 out of 100 |
| Fluency score | Often words read correctly per minute on a grade-level passage |
| Decoding or phonics inventory | A check of which letter-sound patterns a child can read |
| Intervention | Extra targeted instruction beyond regular classroom teaching |

Never hesitate to ask, "Can you explain what that score means for my child?"

## When you and the teacher disagree

Sometimes a parent sees a problem the teacher does not, or the reverse. Stay focused on your child rather than on who is right. Share specific observations, ask what evidence would help clarify the picture, and propose a time-limited plan: "Could we try extra support for six weeks and then look at progress together?" If concerns remain unresolved, you can involve the reading specialist, school counselor or principal, and you have the right to request an evaluation in writing.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I talk to the teacher in front of my child?

It's usually better to have the initial conversation without your child, then involve them appropriately in age-appropriate discussions.

### What if I disagree with the teacher?

Ask for evidence and a plan, involve the school's reading specialist or principal if needed, and remember your right to request an evaluation.

### Can I ask for a meeting with the reading specialist?

Yes. Many schools have reading specialists or interventionists who can provide more detailed information.

## In summary

Reach out early, prepare specific observations and questions, ask about skills, assessment, instruction and support, and agree on a clear plan with follow-up dates. Approach the teacher as a partner, keep communication going, know your right to request an evaluation and keep supporting your child's reading and confidence at home.
