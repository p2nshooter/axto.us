When adults think about children's reading, they often think of stories: picture books, fairy tales and novels. Stories are wonderful. But many children are just as passionate, or more passionate, about facts: sharks, volcanoes, trucks, outer space, how the body works, ancient Egypt, football statistics. Informational books, or nonfiction, satisfy that curiosity and build skills that stories alone cannot.

This guide explains why nonfiction matters, how to choose good informational books by age, how to read them together and how they can help reluctant readers discover the joy of reading.

## Why nonfiction matters

### Background knowledge powers comprehension

Research has repeatedly shown that knowing about a topic makes reading about it much easier. A child who has read about the ocean will understand a story set at sea, a science lesson about tides and a news article about whales more easily than a child who hasn't. Nonfiction builds the broad knowledge of the world that underpins comprehension across every subject.

### Academic vocabulary

Informational books introduce words children need for school: "habitat," "predator," "erosion," "orbit," "ancient," "evidence." These words appear across science, history and social studies texts. Children who meet them early in engaging books are better prepared.

### Different text structures

Nonfiction is organized differently from stories. Children learn to navigate headings, tables of contents, indexes, glossaries, captions, diagrams, maps and timelines, and to recognize structures like cause and effect, compare and contrast, sequence and problem and solution. These skills are essential as reading shifts from learning to read to reading to learn around third grade. See [reading milestones by age](/guides/reading-milestones-by-age/).

### Curiosity and motivation

Many children are naturally drawn to facts. For some, especially those who don't connect with stories, nonfiction is the gateway to loving reading.

## Types of nonfiction for children

| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| Concept books | Colors, shapes, counting for young children |
| Informational picture books | Animals, weather, the body, machines |
| Narrative nonfiction | True stories told like a story: explorers, inventions, historical events |
| Biographies | Lives of scientists, artists, athletes, leaders |
| How-to books | Crafts, cooking, science experiments, drawing |
| Reference books | Children's encyclopedias, atlases, dictionaries |
| Fact books and record books | Lists of amazing facts, record-breakers |
| Graphic nonfiction | Science, history and biography in comic form |

## Choosing good nonfiction

### Accuracy

Check that the book is up to date and written by authors with expertise or careful research. Look for back matter: sources, author's notes, glossaries and further reading.

### Engaging presentation

Good nonfiction uses clear writing, compelling visuals (photographs, diagrams, illustrations) and a voice that invites curiosity rather than lecturing.

### Age-appropriate depth

Young children need simple facts and clear images; older children can handle more detail, complex explanations and nuance.

### Follow their passions

The best nonfiction book for a child is one about a topic they love. Let interests lead.

## Nonfiction by age

| Age | What works |
|---|---|
| 2–4 | Board books with photographs of animals, vehicles and everyday objects; simple concept books |
| 4–6 | Informational picture books with large photos or illustrations and a few sentences per page |
| 6–8 | Early reader nonfiction with controlled text; picture book biographies; how-to books |
| 8–10 | Longer informational books with text features; narrative nonfiction; graphic nonfiction |
| 10–12 | In-depth books on topics of interest; biographies and memoirs; books about science, history and current issues |

## Reading nonfiction with children

Nonfiction doesn't have to be read cover to cover:

- **Browse and dip in.** Let your child choose a page that interests them.
- **Use text features.** Show how to use the table of contents and index to find information. Read captions and labels.
- **Talk about the pictures and diagrams.** "What does this diagram show? Which part surprises you?"
- **Ask questions together.** "What do you want to find out?" Then look for the answer.
- **Connect to experience.** After reading about bugs, go on a bug hunt. After a book about volcanoes, make a baking soda volcano.
- **Explain new words.** Point out academic vocabulary and use it in conversation.

## Nonfiction for reluctant readers

Children who resist stories often respond enthusiastically to facts about things they care about. Record books, fact books, sports books and books about video game design or animals can draw them in. Short sections, lots of visuals and the satisfaction of learning something new make nonfiction approachable. See [how to motivate a reluctant reader](/guides/reluctant-readers/).

## Balancing fiction and nonfiction

Children benefit from both. Stories build empathy, imagination and understanding of narrative; nonfiction builds knowledge, vocabulary and different reading skills. Studies of classroom libraries and read-aloud practices have often found that fiction dominates. Making a conscious effort to include nonfiction in read-alouds and library choices helps balance the diet.

## Nonfiction activities at home

- **Expert of the week:** your child chooses a topic, reads about it and "teaches" the family.
- **Fact-finding missions:** before a trip to the zoo, museum or park, read about what you'll see.
- **Question jar:** collect questions children ask during the week, then find answers together in books.
- **Make a mini book:** your child writes and illustrates their own informational book on a favorite topic, complete with a table of contents and glossary.

## A fiction-nonfiction pairing idea

Pairing a story with a nonfiction book on the same topic deepens understanding and shows children how different kinds of texts work:

| Story | Nonfiction partner |
|---|---|
| A picture book about a bear preparing for winter | An informational book about hibernation |
| A chapter book set on a pirate ship | A nonfiction book about real pirates and ships |
| A story about a child moving to a new country | A book about that country's geography and culture |
| A fantasy about dragons | A book about real reptiles and dinosaurs |

## Teaching children to judge information

Nonfiction is also a natural place to start teaching children to think critically about information, a skill they will need constantly online:

- **Who wrote this, and how do they know?** Look at the author's note and sources.
- **When was it published?** Science and technology books can go out of date.
- **Is it a fact or an opinion?** Point out the difference when you meet one.
- **Do other sources agree?** Compare two books, or a book and a museum display, on the same topic.
- **What do the pictures show, and what might they leave out?** Photographs and diagrams are chosen by people.

Even young children can enjoy playing "fact detective," checking a surprising claim in a second book.

## Myths about nonfiction

**"Nonfiction is dry and boring."** Modern children's nonfiction is often stunning, funny and full of surprises.

**"Reading for fun means stories."** For many children, reading for fun means facts.

**"Nonfiction is only for school."** Following a passion through books is one of the most powerful kinds of reading for pleasure.

## Reading features of nonfiction books

Nonfiction books use special features that children need to learn to navigate. Point them out during shared reading:

| Feature | What it helps readers do |
|---|---|
| Table of contents | Find the chapter on a specific topic |
| Index | Locate pages that mention a particular word |
| Glossary | Look up the meaning of key terms |
| Headings | Predict what a section will be about |
| Captions | Understand what a photo or illustration shows |
| Diagrams and labels | See how parts fit together or how a process works |
| Maps and timelines | Understand where and when things happened |
| Bold words | Notice important vocabulary |

A quick "feature hunt", where your child finds and explains each feature in a new book, builds the skills they will need for school research and textbooks.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it okay if my child only reads nonfiction?

Yes, it is reading. Offer stories too, especially read-alouds, but respect your child's interests.

### How can I tell if a nonfiction book is accurate?

Check the publication date, the author's background, sources listed in the back and reviews from librarians or educators.

### Are fact books with short snippets valuable?

Yes. They build vocabulary and curiosity, and they often motivate reluctant readers.

## In summary

Nonfiction builds the background knowledge and academic vocabulary that power reading comprehension, teaches children to navigate different text structures and satisfies natural curiosity. Choose accurate, engaging books on topics your child loves, read them flexibly, use text features, connect books to experiences and balance fiction with nonfiction. For many children, facts are the doorway to a lifelong love of reading.
